\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The strongest chess programs follow one of two designs. One is alpha-beta search with an efficiently updatable neural-network evaluation (NNUE), exemplified by Stockfish~\cite{stockfish,nasu2018,sf12}, which searches tens of millions of nodes per second on a CPU. The other is Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) guided by a deep policy--value network, the approach of AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018} and of Leela Chess Zero (Lc0)~\cite{lc0,monroe2026}, which evaluates far fewer positions (AlphaZero searched about 60{,}000 per second against Stockfish's 60 million~\cite{silver2018}) and compensates with the quality of each evaluation. Both searches can be run on the same position at the same time, and their answers arbitrated, on any machine with a CPU and a GPU; on consumer systems-on-chip such as Apple's M-series~\cite{applem2}, where the CPU and the GPU share one system memory~\cite{appleunified}, this is compact, and contention for the shared resources becomes its main cost. Whether it adds playing strength to a Stockfish-class CPU search is an empirical question, an instance of a general one: how to split test-time compute between a fast search with a cheap evaluation and a slower, more accurate learned evaluator. We know of no controlled measurement that answers it for chess.

\ifanon An earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\else Our earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\fi{} against the engine's source code and the retained records showed that its central quantitative claims (the ratings, the speed-up and the throughput comparison with Lc0) do not hold: each rating was an average, over opponents, of an assumed opponent rating plus the Elo difference implied by a five-game score; the alpha-beta component is Stockfish's own search; and several implementation statements were wrong (Section~\ref{sec:chapter} lists the main ones\ifanon\else; a Correction has been prepared\fi). This paper is the controlled study the chapter lacked. It takes the engine, updates it as described in Section~\ref{sec:prep}, freezes it, and asks one pre-registered question with everything else held equal:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does running an Lc0-class transformer MCTS on the GPU concurrently with a Stockfish-class alpha-beta search on the CPU, with a final arbiter, change playing strength relative to the alpha-beta search alone, at equal wall-clock and equal CPU-thread budget on one machine?}
\end{quote}

Equal CPU-thread budget means the same \texttt{Threads} setting for both arms; the hybrid's single MCTS worker runs as one extra thread that mostly waits on the GPU. ``Lc0-class'' refers to the network, Lc0's BT4 transformer, not to Lc0's search configuration, which batches evaluations over several threads (Section~\ref{sec:system}). The estimand is the score of Hybrid(8) against AB(8) from unbalanced human openings at 30~s plus 0.3~s per move, on one Apple M2 Max under the logged host load, with the shipped hybrid (one MCTS worker at batch size~1) as the treatment. In the frozen engine the GPU search can change the outcome through three channels: the MCTS root hints and the verification sub-search, which act on every move with the transformer enabled; contention for CPU time and memory bandwidth; and the arbiter's override, which in the gating matches before the freeze never selected the MCTS move (0 of 1{,}344 disagreements in the frozen build's gate L14, 0 of 1{,}165 in the pilot). RQ1a therefore mostly measures the first two channels, and whether arbitration could be worth anything is the question of the oracle study (RQ6b). %src: results/paper_v2/sprt/L14_hybrid_lean_vs_ab8_30p03/moves.jsonl; results/paper_v2/RQ1a_pilot/moves.jsonl
The answer is measured as an Elo difference with a confidence interval; for the earlier split configuration, the isolation cells of RQ2 divide the effect into the cost of two alpha-beta threads and the value the GPU search returns; and the oracle study explains it by what the two searches disagree about and who is right when they do. The design was fixed in a protocol merged into the public repository before the first confirmatory game, and every later amendment carries its own merge time (Section~\ref{sec:amend}); the engine was frozen at a tagged commit; every number is traced to a retained artifact, and every table and figure is generated from the artifacts by a script.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled methodology, fixed in advance in a time-stamped public protocol, for evaluating hybrid or ensemble chess engines that share one machine: a fair-resource policy, telemetry of when the GPU path is actually enabled, host-load logging with a scheduling gate, component isolation cells and an oracle analysis, combined with the conventions adopted from Fishtest (colour-swapped pairs, unbalanced human openings, pentanomial statistics with normalised Elo), with all artifacts released (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A corrected as-built description of \MF{}, the system first reported in~\cite{niketan2026chapter}: a concurrent alpha-beta plus MCTS runtime with shared root state, MCTS-to-alpha-beta root-ordering hints and a gated arbiter, with the BT4 transformer on Metal Performance Shaders Graph; the new element is the measurement of its batch-1 latency ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:system}) and of the contention between the two searches inside games (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Alpha-beta engines}
Stockfish combines alpha-beta pruning~\cite{knuth1975} with aspiration windows, null-move and futility pruning, late-move reductions and a shared transposition table, and since 2020 it has evaluated positions with an NNUE network~\cite{nasu2018,sf12}, whose first layer is updated incrementally as moves are made and unmade. Its development is driven by Fishtest, a distributed testing framework whose statistical conventions (game pairs with colour swap, unbalanced opening books, pentanomial statistics, sequential tests) are designed to measure small strength differences~\cite{fishtest,stockfish,sfbooks}. The two designs are not independent in what they know: Stockfish's networks have been trained partly on positions evaluated by Leela Chess Zero since Stockfish 14~\cite{sf14}, and all of Stockfish 19's training positions were rescored with a strong Leela network~\cite{stockfish}.

\subsection{Neural-network MCTS engines}
MCTS~\cite{coulom2006,kocsis2006,browne2012} with PUCT selection~\cite{rosin2011} and a policy--value network, as in AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018}, grows a search tree whose leaves are evaluated by the network, so its quality depends on how many evaluations fit into the time for a move. Lc0 is an open-source engine in the AlphaZero tradition whose current networks are transformers~\cite{monroe2026,lc0}; the BT4 network used here is one of them. Transformers have also been trained to play chess without explicit search~\cite{ruoss2024}. On a given GPU the evaluation rate is governed by batching: at small batch sizes, fixed framework overhead dominates inference time~\cite{fernandez2023}; Section~\ref{sec:system} measures latency against batch size for the backend used here, without attributing it to its components. Batched MCTS keeps its tree statistics usable by marking in-flight nodes with a virtual loss~\cite{chaslot2008}. Co-executing one workload on the CPU and the GPU of a unified-memory Apple chip helps in some phases of language-model inference and not in others~\cite{mohite2026}.

\subsection{Combining minimax and MCTS}
The algorithmic literature on hybrids embeds one search inside the other. Baier and Winands embed shallow minimax searches in the selection, rollout and backpropagation phases of MCTS~\cite{baier2013,baier2015} and later add static state evaluations~\cite{baier2018}; Lanctot et al.\ back up an implicit minimax value alongside the Monte Carlo average~\cite{lanctot2014}; Huang shows that alpha-beta and MT-SSS* can be expressed as rollout algorithms~\cite{huang2015}, and Baier unifies MCTS and alpha-beta in one rollout-based algorithm~\cite{baier2017}. Ji and Thielscher vary the depth of the embedded minimax search dynamically~\cite{ji2023}. A common motivation for these hybrids is the weakness of sampling-based search in tactical positions: UCT can miss shallow traps that minimax finds~\cite{ramanujan2010}, and in synthetic chess-like games it can show lookahead pathology, although minimax is similarly susceptible in that setting~\cite{nguyen2024}. In the AlphaZero setting, Chen et al.\ add an alpha-beta phase to an AlphaZero-style player~\cite{chen2019}, Helfenstein et al.\ combine a mixture of experts with MCTS in chess~\cite{helfenstein2024}, and Cohen-Solal and Cazenave show that minimax-based learning remains competitive with MCTS-based learning~\cite{cohen2023}.

\subsection{Engines that combine or compare the paradigms}
Engine practice has its own precedents. Scorpio offers MCTS with alpha-beta rollouts and, with neural networks, a network-guided MCTS that uses alpha-beta searches as a prior~\cite{scorpio}; Komodo, since version 12, has an MCTS mode whose tree is expanded best-first from tiny alpha-beta searches instead of random playouts or a network~\cite{komodo}; a 2017 Stockfish branch experimented with a Monte Carlo search on top of Stockfish's evaluation~\cite{sfmcts2017}; the lc0-stockfish-hybrid project uses Stockfish as a filter inside Lc0's selection~\cite{lc0sfhybrid}; Ceres and Allie are independent MCTS searches on Lc0 networks~\cite{ceres,allie}; Stoofvlees II, an MCTS engine whose design departs from the AlphaZero recipe, won the 2023 World Computer Chess Championship~\cite{pascutto2025}; and ShashChess has an experimental option that applies Monte Carlo tree search, with its own threads, in selected position types~\cite{shashchess}. Adjacent work models human play across skill levels with a neural policy~\cite{maia2} and combines such policies with engine search~\cite{carlson2026}, for goals other than playing strength.

\subsection{Choosing among several programs' moves}
Selecting a move from the proposals of several programs predates neural networks. Alth\"ofer's multiple-choice systems give a human or an automatic decider the candidate moves of two or more programs~\cite{althofer2003,althofer2004}; Clever~\&~Smart, an automatic version in which two versions of Shredder passed their proposals and search data to a control program that chose the move and managed the clock, finished second at the 1998 International Paderborn Computer Chess Championship~\cite{cleversmart}. In computer shogi, consultation by majority vote among several programs~\cite{obata2011} and an optimistic rule that plays the move of the program reporting the best evaluation~\cite{sugiyama2011} were compared in controlled matches, and consultation was used in the distributed system Akara 2010~\cite{hoki2013}. In computer Go, a team of diverse, individually weaker programs can outplay a uniform team of stronger ones~\cite{marcolino2013,marcolino2014}, and root parallelisation combines independent MCTS searches at the root~\cite{chaslot2008}. The hybrid studied here is a two-member consultation between searches of different paradigms, run concurrently on one chip under an equal \texttt{Threads} setting; with two members a majority vote cannot break a disagreement, and the arbiter resolves it by defaulting to the alpha-beta move unless gated predicates favour the MCTS move, which is closer to a veto rule than to the optimistic rule. What is new is not the consultation but a controlled, equal-budget measurement of it, together with a bound on what any such arbiter could gain.

\subsection{Measuring strength}
Elo differences between near-equal engines are small relative to game-to-game noise, and the measurement conventions matter as much as the games. Fishtest's methodology, documented by Van den Bergh~\cite{vdbergh_gsprt,vdbergh_rw,vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}, plays colour-swapped game pairs from unbalanced human openings (UHO)~\cite{sfbooks,pohl2024} so that draws do not swamp the signal, and reports pentanomial statistics and normalised Elo; the book choice itself is debated~\cite{uhocritique}. Draw modelling in Elo~\cite{szczecinski2020} and uncertainty estimation in paired-comparison experiments~\cite{glickman1999} are the statistical background; Bayesian Elo~\cite{bayeselo} and Ordo~\cite{ordo} fit static paired-comparison models to multi-engine results (Ordo is the tool used here), and whole-history rating~\cite{coulom2008} extends such models to players whose strength changes over time. Ratings from public lists such as CCRL are specific to their reference hardware and time control~\cite{ccrl} and cannot be imported to another machine; in its seventeenth season the Top Chess Engine Championship gave CPU and GPU engines separate lower leagues, each on its own hardware, before both met in the Premier Division~\cite{haworth2020}. Test-position suites, mostly tactical (the Strategic Test Suite is not)~\cite{bratko1982,sts,eret,arasan,lichesspuzzles}, measure something other than playing strength and, for an engine that was tuned on them, measure it optimistically.

\section{The system as built}
\label{sec:system}

\MF{} is a single UCI binary with three modes selected by options: plain alpha-beta (\texttt{AB}), stand-alone transformer MCTS, and the hybrid that runs both concurrently. \subsection{Alpha-beta component}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish's search and NNUE evaluation. At the freeze it is a port of Stockfish 19 with its SFNNv16 network (\texttt{nn-1a298aa575a0.nnue}, 98.5 MB); the engine's \texttt{bench} command reproduces the upstream signature of 2{,}497{,}913 nodes, so the search is node-for-node Stockfish's. %src: results/paper_v2/queue_paper_log.txt (bench lines 25, 60, 98, 105); upstream tag sf_19; 03 §0
Its local changes are few: the root accepts move-ordering hints from the MCTS component, the previous principal variation is kept after an unproven fail-high, the hybrid can set the size of the thread pool, and there are an AArch64 spin-wait hint, two branch-likelihood hints, thread quality-of-service classes, a de-duplication of \texttt{searchmoves}, a transposition-table allocation check and a NEON kernel. There is no GPU code in the alpha-beta search; NNUE runs on the CPU by design, because incremental CPU evaluation is faster than any GPU round trip at the granularity of a node. On the test machine the search reaches 1.53 million nodes/s on one thread, 11.81 million on eight threads and 13.52 million on all twelve cores at fixed depth (Figure~\ref{fig:m2}; measured on 13 October 2026 with \ifanon a pre-freeze build\else build \texttt{8777b99}\fi{} whose search code is the frozen one; the re-run on the frozen binary follows the cells). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/ab_scaling.json (depth 16, median of 5)

\subsection{MCTS component and neural-network backend}
The MCTS component follows Lc0's search: PUCT selection with first-play urgency, a policy softmax temperature, tree reuse between moves and a direct-mapped cache of network outputs keyed on the position; it implements Lc0's algorithm and parameters and reads Lc0's network format, and is not claimed as a contribution. Nodes are 128 bytes and aligned to the M-series cache line; a 4-byte \texttt{os\_unfair\_lock} protects score updates. The BT4 transformer (\texttt{BT4-1024x15x32h}, 15 encoder blocks, 32 heads, 1024-wide; 383 MB of weights) is built once as a Metal Performance Shaders graph~\cite{mpsgraph} with an FP16 body and FP32 inputs and outputs, and encoded into a command buffer for each evaluation; one evaluation is in flight at a time. The stand-alone MCTS mode runs one worker at batch size 1; the hybrid runs one worker.

Batch-1 inference is the structural ceiling of this design. On the M2 Max with no game running (13 October 2026, before host load was logged; \ifanon a pre-freeze build\else build \texttt{8777b99}\fi{} whose network code is the frozen one) a single BT4 evaluation takes 7.40 ms in FP16 (135 positions/s) and 8.74 ms in FP32; a batch of 8 takes 19.7 ms (407 positions/s) and a batch of 64 takes 119 ms (538 positions/s), so batching could raise throughput up to fourfold (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). The engine as shipped does not batch; whether batching, with virtual loss keeping the tree statistics usable~\cite{chaslot2008}, would help it inside a game is not tested here (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency.json (2026-10-13 sweep, queue_paper_log.txt; to be refreshed from nn_latency_frozen.json)
A search with Lc0 0.32.1's Metal backend and the same weights reaches 165 nodes/s at minibatch 1 and at best 725 nodes/s (minibatch 32) on the same machine. \MF{}'s evaluation rate is therefore below Lc0's search rate at batch size 1, the setting its search uses, and at each program's best batch size (538 against 725 per second); at batch sizes 2 to 8 it is above, at 16 and 32 below and at 64 about equal (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). The two tools count different things (network evaluations against search nodes), so these comparisons are indicative only. %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency.json (lc0 nps_steady_median)
Inside a game the MCTS worker competes with eight alpha-beta threads for memory bandwidth and scheduling; its observed rate is reported with the game telemetry in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Hybrid runtime and arbiter}
\label{sec:arbiter}
On every move the coordinator starts the alpha-beta search and the MCTS search on the same position. Alpha-beta keeps all $T$ threads and the single MCTS worker runs as an extra thread that mostly blocks on the GPU (\texttt{HybridOversubscribe}). Stockfish's clock-based time manager owns the move (\texttt{HybridFollowABTime}): the coordinator ends the move as soon as the alpha-beta search returns and stops the MCTS search, which is otherwise limited by the coordinator's own per-move budget (the remaining clock divided by 30 plus three quarters of the increment, within fixed bounds) less the move overhead. The two searches publish their root state through two cache-line-aligned structures whose fields are written with relaxed atomic stores and made visible through a release-ordered flag and counter; the decision reads them after both searches have stopped, which each thread signals with a release-ordered completion flag. If a search has not stopped within a grace period (a quarter of the budget, between 1 and 5~s), the coordinator decides without the MCTS statistics; in the gating match L14 this never happened. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 468-500, 2849-2855, 4037-4042, 4124-4141; hybrid_search.h 36-141; L14 engines.log.gz ("subsearch stop grace expired": 0)

At the start of a move the alpha-beta side reads up to eight moves from the MCTS root, which at that moment holds the subtree kept from the previous move, or nothing. These hints only reorder alpha-beta's initial list of root moves, so every thread searches the first hint first in the first iteration; from the second iteration Stockfish orders the root by score as usual, the aspiration windows are unaffected, and nothing else in the search reads the hints. When the coordinator's budget is at least one second, the first five hints are first verified by a restricted alpha-beta search of $\min(240~\text{ms}, \text{budget}/8)$; with a queen on the board, a further check of 80 to 160~ms can follow for each verified candidate that is a quiet rook or queen check (at most four). The verification shares the transposition and history tables with the main search, and its result feeds two of the predicates below. Its time is spent from the game clock before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move, and it replaces state that the time manager carries from one search to the next, so the hybrid's alpha-beta search does not divide the clock exactly as the alpha-beta arm does (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 572-597, 3102-3176, 3537-3669, 3829-3864; search/search.cpp 59-98, 275-277

When both searches name the same move, it is played, except that two tie-break rules can play a third move from the MCTS list when its prior or its value strongly favours it. When they disagree, the arbiter plays alpha-beta's move unless a predicate selects the MCTS move: 36 predicates are tested in a fixed order, and 31 of them can fire under the tested settings (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}). A veto blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks its own move first and the MCTS move lower, and either its average score for the MCTS move trails by at least 30 internal units with at least 10{,}000 nodes spent on its own move, or it barely searched the MCTS move. Of the 31 predicates, 24 override the veto, two do so only under stricter conditions, four are blocked by it, and one applies only when alpha-beta has not reached depth 10, when there is no veto. The veto encodes a design assumption: with a few hundred MCTS visits against millions of alpha-beta nodes, a move that alpha-beta has searched to depth 10 and found worse by a margin is more often a tactical oversight of the shallow MCTS search than a long-term judgement that alpha-beta misses. The oracle study tests this assumption on the disagreements (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}). The pawn-lever and quiet-attack tie-break overrides, which were added after the chapter, are off. In the gating matches before the freeze the arbiter never played the MCTS move (0 of 1{,}344 disagreements in L14). A time-safety rule disables the transformer for a move when the estimated budget is below 400~ms or the remaining clock below 3~s; the move is then plain alpha-beta with Stockfish's own output, and the engine logs the rule but writes no decision trace. The position classifier that the chapter described as weighting the decision can only lengthen the coordinator's budget, never alpha-beta's search time; a longer budget lengthens the MCTS search, the verification cap and the grace period. An agreement rule ends a move early when both searches have named the same move in three consecutive polls after a quarter of the budget, with at least 100 MCTS nodes and alpha-beta at depth 10; it fired on 3 of the 3{,}278 transformer-enabled moves of the 30+0.3 pilot and on none of the 3{,}442 in L14, and its time saving has not been measured. %src: results/paper_v2/RQ1a_pilot/engines.log.gz and L14 engines.log.gz ("engines agree, stopping early"); 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 1860-1873, 4058-4082, 4946-4951, 5288-5306, 5366-5480

With \texttt{HybridTrace} on, as in every hybrid arm, the coordinator writes one decision line per move after both searches have stopped and before \texttt{bestmove}, so the trace cannot affect either search; its cost counts towards the move's time and was not measured. The line records the reason, both candidates, their scores and visit counts, every predicate's value, the MCTS top ten and alpha-beta's top sixteen root moves; the recorded reason is the first predicate in ladder order that holds, which need not be the one that lifted the veto. The score the hybrid reports before \texttt{bestmove} is alpha-beta's (Stockfish's normalised centipawns, with mates as $\pm(30000-\text{plies})$ centipawns) unless it plays the MCTS move, when it reports $300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$, bounded at $\pm 1140$; time-safety moves report Stockfish's own output. At the first \texttt{isready} the engine loads the network, builds the Metal graph and runs one batch-1 warm-up evaluation before it answers \texttt{readyok}, which the match manager awaits before the game starts, so the game clock does not pay for initialisation. The complete list of predicates and thresholds, with source references, is in the arbiter reference \ifanon in the archive~\depositcite{}\else in the repository (\texttt{docs/paper\_v2/arbiter\_reference.md})\fi. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 4147-4214, 5483-5590; uci.cpp 161-164, 1579-1622, 1727-1737; mcts/search.cpp 278-322; L14 engines.log.gz (isready/readyok before the first go)

\subsection{Engine preparation and freeze}
\label{sec:prep}
The study started from the engine as of July 2026, which already differed from the version the chapter described (May 2026): for example, the transformer's low-time fallback was then a fixed 5~s, and the tie-break overrides did not exist yet. Before the freeze, a short engineering programme (exploratory, reported in full in the programme record~\depositcite{}) made four changes, each adopted on the evidence of a gating match: the first two against the preceding binary, the third as an option toggle within one binary, and the fourth by comparing the capped and the uncapped hybrid against the same alpha-beta opponent. Here and below, $\pm$ is half the width of the 95\% pentanomial interval. The alpha-beta core was moved from a January 2026 Stockfish development snapshot to Stockfish 19 (+\elo{62.0}{28.2} over the old base at 10+0.1, 136 games); %src: 08 §A sf19port_vs_preport_ab_10p01_v2

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Principles and fair-resource policy}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish, so its strength is not a contribution and is never compared across machines. Every primary comparison is \emph{same binary, same machine, same wall-clock, same \texttt{Threads} setting}, with the GPU search switched on or off; the \texttt{Threads} setting is a nominal thread budget, because the hybrid runs its MCTS worker as one extra, mostly GPU-blocked thread (amendment~3). Alpha-beta was not also run with more threads to occupy the cores that thread uses, because the thread mostly waits on the GPU and the host was shared; the protocol's thread-scaling cells were not run. The protocol~\depositcite{} fixed the hypotheses, cells, sample sizes, statistics and decision rules before data collection: its frozen version was merged into the public repository on 14 October 2026 at 06:46~UTC\ifanon\else{} (pull request~\#77, a server-side time stamp)\fi, and the first confirmatory game started at 08:09~UTC. It was amended only by numbered entries written before the affected cell was interpreted (thirteen amendments, each with its merge time; Section~\ref{sec:amend}). All games, the engine logs of the cells with a hybrid arm, traces, manifests with hashes and seeds, and thermal logs are retained, and every figure and table is generated from them by a script.

\subsection{Environment}
All measurements were taken on one Apple M2 Max (8 performance and 4 efficiency CPU cores, 38-core GPU, 32 GB unified memory) running macOS 27.2 and prevented from sleeping. The system installed its updates automatically, so the cells ran on pre-release builds of macOS 27.2 (beta, beta~3 from 16 October, the release candidate from 20 October) and the last blocks on the final release (from 31 October); the build installed at each moment is recorded, and every cell is also reported by build (amendment~13). The protocol assumed an otherwise idle machine; the host was in fact shared with other work, so the CPU used by other processes was logged every minute and games were scheduled around it (amendments 8--10 in Section~\ref{sec:amend}); from 27 October 2026 the GPU's utilisation and memory and the host's memory pressure were also logged every minute (amendment~12). Games were managed by fastchess v1.8.2-alpha at concurrency 1. Reference engines were Stockfish 19 (tag \texttt{sf\_19}, PGO build) and Lc0 0.32.1 with the Metal backend and the same BT4 weights. Syzygy 3--4--5 tablebases were available only to the oracle judges of Section~\ref{sec:oracle}, never to the engines under test. The engine was built with Apple clang 21.0.0 against the macOS 27.0 SDK as a C++20 release build with link-time optimisation and \texttt{-mcpu=native}, with its Homebrew libraries bundled; the BT4 weights file has SHA-256 \texttt{8accc462\ldots{}508e}, and the frozen binary's SHA-256 is recorded in every manifest. Stockfish 19, the reference, is a PGO build.

\subsection{Match protocol}
\label{sec:match}
Each cell is a fixed number of game pairs: every opening is played twice with colours swapped. Openings are drawn in a seeded random order from Fishtest's \texttt{UHO\_Lichess\_4852\_v1} book of 2{,}632{,}036 unbalanced human positions~\cite{sfbooks}; the same seed is used in every cell of a research question, so arms and cells see the same opening sample. Engines are restarted for every game and do not ponder; the \MF{} arms use a 50 ms move overhead (the protocol's setting after a 12 ms overrun at 10+0.1 with the 10 ms default), and the reference engines keep their defaults (10 ms for Stockfish 19). Games are adjudicated as drawn after move 34 when both engines' scores have stayed within $\pm 20$ cp for eight consecutive moves each (Fishtest's draw rule) and as lost after three consecutive scores beyond $\pm 600$ cp from both engines' perspectives, and they are capped at 200 moves; adjudicated games, time forfeits and crashes are counted and reported per cell, and no game is excluded after the fact. The rules read the last score each engine reports before its move; for the hybrid this is alpha-beta's score except on the rare moves where it plays the MCTS move (Section~\ref{sec:arbiter}). Each cell is split into four blocks of equal size that draw disjoint openings; the blocks of the four primary cells are interleaved round-robin over the calendar so that thermal and background drift are balanced across them, and later cells run block after block; the thermal state is logged every minute. The hybrid's telemetry (\texttt{HybridTrace}) records, for every move, whether the transformer was active, the MCTS playouts and evaluations, both candidates, and the arbiter's decision and reason.

\subsection{Statistics}
The primary estimator is the pentanomial model: each game pair scores 0, $\tfrac12$, 1, $1\tfrac12$ or 2 points, and the mean and variance of the pair score give the logistic Elo difference with a 95\% normal-approximation confidence interval, the likelihood of superiority (LOS), and normalised Elo (nElo), which divides the score excess by its per-game standard deviation, so that the number of games needed to resolve a difference does not depend on the draw rate or the opening book~\cite{vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}. The statistics are computed independently from the PGN by our own script and must agree with fastchess's report to rounding. Multi-engine tables use Ordo~\cite{ordo} anchored to Stockfish 19 at 0 on this hardware; no absolute rating is claimed. The oracle study's intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples games as clusters within strata (10{,}000 resamples), and its rates over all disagreements are reweighted by the stratum sizes. Block consistency is tested with Cochran's $Q$ over the four block estimates, with a block bootstrap as a robustness interval that resamples the runner's chunks, the contiguous sessions of play between scheduling pauses. The derived estimate of RQ2c (RQ2b plus RQ2a) comes from cells that share their openings, so its variance includes the covariance of the two cells' per-opening pair scores. Besides the exclusion analysis of amendment~8, every cell is reported with a pair-level regression of the pair score on the fraction of its minutes under high outside load (giving the estimate at zero load), with the pairs that contain a time forfeit removed, and with forfeits scored as draws; RQ1a is also reported without its first 92 openings, which the pilot and the set-aside games had already played (amendment~12). A result that moves by more than one standard error under any of these is called load-sensitive. Every cell is also reported by the macOS build on which each of its pairs started (amendment~13; for RQ1a this breakdown was added after its interpretation). RQ1a at 30+0.3 is the single confirmatory test; all other cells are estimates with confidence intervals and are labelled exploratory, no family-wise correction is applied, and every planned cell is reported whether or not it ran to completion.

\subsection{Research questions and cells}
\label{sec:rqs}
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (confirmatory for RQ1a only).] Does the GPU search change strength at an equal \texttt{Threads} setting? Hybrid(8) vs AB(8) at 30~s plus 0.3~s per move, 1{,}000 pairs (RQ1a); 60~s plus 0.6~s per move, 500 pairs (RQ1b, exploratory: the regime with the most GPU participation). The decision rule is the sign of the 95\% interval of RQ1a; an interval spanning zero is reported as inconclusive at this $N$, together with the range of effects it excludes. What each outcome means was fixed before reading (amendment~12): an interval above zero means the concurrent GPU search adds strength at equal \texttt{Threads} on this machine, with RQ6 and the telemetry saying through which channel; an interval spanning zero means that no effect was detected, so the GPU path does not pay for itself at this budget on this machine; an interval below zero means that the hybrid costs strength, with RQ2, the telemetry and the host-load analyses bounding how much of that is thread or contention cost.
  \item[RQ2 (isolation, split configuration only).] What do two threads cost and what does the GPU search return given six threads? AB(6) vs AB(8) (RQ2a) and Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(6) (RQ2b), 500 pairs each at 30+0.3; Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(8) (RQ2c) is their combination. The decomposition applies to the split design, not to the shipped oversubscribed hybrid.
  \item[RQ5 (validity checks).] Is the alpha-beta core at parity with Stockfish 19 (AB(8) vs SF19(8) at 30+0.3, 500 pairs; RQ5c, read as a parity interval with no equivalence claim), and is the MCTS implementation at parity with Lc0 at matched settings (MCTS(1) vs Lc0-mb1, 300 pairs; RQ5a, not part of this version)?
  \item[RQ6 (arbitration).] From the traces: disagreement rate, visits at decision time, decision reasons, and how often the MCTS move is chosen (RQ6a). An oracle study on 600 disagreement positions, sampled by stratum (game phase and MCTS visit count) and never by a judge's verdict, judges both candidates with two engines whose lineages overlap one candidate each: Stockfish 19 (8 threads, 60~s, both moves scored at equal depth with multi-PV, tablebases), which runs the alpha-beta candidate's search with more time, and Lc0 with the BT4 network, the MCTS candidate's network, which scores each candidate with equal effort by evaluating the position after it with 30{,}000 nodes from a fresh search tree, with Lc0's early stopping (smart pruning) off so that every search uses the full budget. It reports, per judge, the fraction of positions where each candidate is better and the expected-score loss of each move (a logistic conversion with 250~cp per unit of log-odds), the judges' agreement, and the bound on what an arbiter choosing between the two root candidates at decision time could gain, both per judge and on the positions where the judges agree; its projected Elo is the logistic Elo of the expected-score difference (RQ6b). An ablation with the MCTS-to-alpha-beta hints and the verification sub-search off together (RQ6d, against AB(8) at 30+0.3, 500 pairs) measures that combined channel; its comparison with RQ1a is an across-match contrast reported with propagated uncertainty.
  \item[Microbenchmarks.] BT4 latency and throughput against batch size for \MF{} and Lc0 (M1), alpha-beta nodes/s against threads (M2), and, from the game traces, in-game MCTS evaluations per second, transformer-enabled fraction and playouts per move against time control (M3 and M5); M4, the memory footprint, is in Section~\ref{sec:prep}.
\end{description}
The labels follow the protocol. Its further cells (RQ3 and RQ4: CPU-budget crossover at 4 and 6 threads and MCTS batch-size scaling; RQ5b; RQ6c; a balanced-book control, a blitz cell, suites and a multi-engine Ordo table) are listed in the released manifest; those not run are reported as not run. RQ6c, an ablation of the predicate ladder, needs an arbitration switch that the frozen build does not have (the engine ignores the option the protocol names), so it cannot be run on this binary.

The pilot found the memory defect described in Section~\ref{sec:prep}, which was fixed before the freeze; the pilot games are reported as a diagnostic and are not part of RQ1a. Twelve amendments were recorded, each before the affected cell was interpreted: (1) the hybrid arm is the shipped configuration after the time-management and tie-break changes; (2) engines restart per game; (3) the shipped hybrid is oversubscribed, so the split configuration became the ablation of RQ2; (4) the transformer-enabled fraction at 30+0.3 (about 50\% of hybrid moves) fell short of the 80\% feasibility target, which was kept as a covariate and led to RQ1b being promoted; (5) RQ6d was promoted after the pilot; (6) the strata of the oracle study were defined; (7) a system-library upgrade on the host broke the frozen binary during the first block of RQ1a, so the same source was rebuilt, re-verified (bench signature, unit suite, network reference outputs; the batch-parity check matched up to batch 8, and at batch 16 one FP16 policy logit differed by 0.0056 against a tolerance of 0.005, a batch size the engine does not use) and re-frozen with its dynamic libraries bundled, and RQ1a restarted from its first opening with the 183 earlier games set aside; (8) because the host was shared with other work, blocks are played in chunks that start only when the CPU used by other processes, averaged over five minutes, is below three cores (the engines need about nine of the twelve; block 0 of RQ1a and RQ2a had been played before the gate existed), and every cell is also reported with the pairs that overlapped a minute above three cores removed (Section~\ref{sec:threats}); (9) after four time forfeits during a load spike inside a chunk, chunks were shortened from ten pairs to four and a watchdog stops a running chunk at the next completed pair when the two-minute external load exceeds 4.5 cores; (10) after a further forfeit during a spike below that trigger, the watchdog uses the gate's threshold of three cores, and time forfeits are reported per arm (Table~\ref{tab:health}); (11) an interim reading of each cell has been recorded after every block for monitoring, beyond the running fastchess printout the protocol allowed, with no decision rule attached (this amendment discloses the practice; amendments 8--10 were written after the first reading); (12) after a review of the design with every result withheld, the reading rules of RQ1a and RQ5c, the meaning of each outcome, the further sensitivity analyses of Section~\ref{sec:method}, the GPU and memory covariates, the oracle's judging design and the description of RQ6d were fixed before any cell was read; (13) the host's update history showed that the cells ran on four automatically installed builds of macOS 27.2 (three pre-release builds and, for the last blocks of RQ2a, RQ2b and RQ5c, the final release, whose installation caused one forfeit in RQ2a), so every cell is also reported by build and the microbenchmarks with their builds. Exceeding the 0.5\% forfeit limit led to scheduling amendments (9 and 10), never to the exclusion of games. The measurements were interrupted by host reboots, including kernel panics, and paused whenever the gate was closed; every block resumed from the next unused opening of the same seeded order, and the manifests record every invocation of every block. No game was deleted. A game whose colour-swapped partner was not played enters no pair and its opening is replayed, except once: before a bookkeeping fix, a watchdog stop split a pair and its opening was skipped, so RQ1a has one pair fewer than planned.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Single platform and what generalises.} Every measurement comes from one M2 Max, and the CUDA build of the same engine is out of scope. The equal-budget accounting, the oracle method and the mechanism (how often, and through which channel, the GPU search affects the move) carry over to other hardware; the absolute Elo, the M2 Max and batch-1 inference do not. A later Apple chip with a larger GPU, or a discrete GPU with batched inference, would need the transformer-enabled fraction, the in-game evaluation rate and RQ1 measured again. \textbf{An under-provisioned GPU arm.} GPU MCTS is normally run with several workers and batched, overlapping evaluations; the arm studied here runs one worker at batch size~1 with one evaluation in flight, and only on the moves where the transformer is enabled, so the in-game evaluations per move are reported per cell next to the rates this chip reaches with batching (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). A null result would not show that a better-driven GPU search cannot help. \textbf{Opening book.} UHO openings make differences measurable but exaggerate Elo spreads relative to balanced books; nElo is reported for that reason and the book critique applies~\cite{uhocritique}. \textbf{Transformer-enabled fraction.} The shipped time-safety rule is part of the system under test; at 30+0.3 the transformer is enabled on about half of the hybrid's moves, and at 10+0.1 it would be enabled on almost none because the move budget is mostly below 400 ms, so results at short time controls are largely alpha-beta results. The fraction is reported descriptively per cell; how the effect depends on GPU participation is read from the time-control cells, not from variation within a cell, and no statement is made beyond the time controls tested (in the AlphaZero study, AlphaZero's strength grew faster with thinking time than Stockfish's~\cite{silver2018}). \textbf{Tuning leakage.} The hybrid and MCTS heuristics were tuned on Bratko--Kopec and Lichess puzzle samples; any suite result for \MF{} is optimistic and is labelled so; game results are unaffected. \textbf{Reference drift.} Stockfish 19 is the alpha-beta base and the parity reference; the gap to the chapter-era base is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:prep}. \textbf{Adjudication.} The draw and resignation rules end a game only when both engines' own scores agree that it is decided; the 200-move cap ends a game regardless of the scores, and capped games are included in the adjudication counts. The protocol's re-run of one RQ1a block without adjudication has not been run for this version. \textbf{Move overhead.} The \MF{} arms run with a 50 ms move overhead and Stockfish 19 with its 10 ms default, so in RQ5c the \MF{} arm has 40 ms less per move; this favours Stockfish 19 slightly and is part of that cell as pre-registered, as are its PGO build against \MF{}'s LTO build. \textbf{Clock division.} Both arms play on the same game clock, which is the resource the comparison holds equal, but they divide it differently: on transformer-enabled moves the hint snapshot and the verification sub-search run before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move, so such a move can take longer than Stockfish would allot on that clock, and the verification replaces state that the time manager carries from one search to the next. The extra time is drawn from the hybrid's own clock and is part of the system under test; the time of every move is recorded in the released games. \textbf{Power and energy.} CPU and GPU power and energy per game were not measured, because the tools need administrator rights on this host; the fair-resource policy counts threads and wall-clock time, not energy. \textbf{Shared host.} The machine was not idle. Over the \HostLogSamples{} minutes logged from \HostLogFirst{} to \HostLogLast{}, other processes used a median of \HostLogMedianCores{} cores and more than three cores in \HostLogOverPct\% of minutes; over the \HostGameSamples{} minutes that overlapped a game, the median was \HostGameMedianCores{} cores (95th percentile \HostGamePninetyfiveCores{}) and \HostGameOverPct\% of those minutes exceeded three cores (the gate checks the load only before a chunk starts, and until amendment~10 the watchdog stopped a chunk only above 4.5 cores). Because the arms of RQ1a and RQ2 need different numbers of free cores, load can bias as well as blur them: time forfeits came in minutes of high outside load wherever the load was logged, and fell mostly on the alpha-beta arm, which favours the hybrid in RQ1a (amendment~10; Table~\ref{tab:health}); every cell is therefore reported with and without the pairs that overlapped a minute of more than three cores of outside load (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). \textbf{System updates.} The engine binary and its bundled libraries never changed, but the operating system it ran on did: four builds of macOS 27.2 were in use over the measurement period, installed automatically. RQ1a ran on the first three; the last update, which forced a restart, came during the final blocks of the other three primary cells and caused one time forfeit in RQ2a. The transformer runs on system frameworks (Metal and MPSGraph), so a build change could alter its speed; the cells are reported by build and the microbenchmarks with their builds. \textbf{Interruptions.} RQ1a was restarted once on a rebuilt binary, and the cells were paused and resumed many times by the load gate and by host reboots (Section~\ref{sec:amend}); per-block results are reported so that any effect of the gaps is visible. \textbf{Statistics.} One confirmatory test at fixed $N$; everything else is exploratory and reported in full.

\section{Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}
\begin{description}
  \item[Time control 30+0.3.] Thirty seconds per player for the game, plus 0.3~s added after each of that player's moves.
  \item[Nodes, playouts, evaluations.] An alpha-beta node is a position visited by the search; an MCTS playout is one descent from the root to a leaf; an evaluation is one network inference, and a playout can reuse a cached evaluation.
  \item[Fail-high.] A search result at or above the upper bound of its search window; it is unproven until a re-search with a wider window confirms it.
  \item[Bench signature.] The node count of an engine's fixed benchmark search; identical counts mean identical search behaviour on those positions.
  \item[PUCT and first-play urgency.] PUCT selects the child that maximises its value plus an exploration term weighted by the network's prior; first-play urgency is the value assumed for children that have not been visited yet.
  \item[Multi-PV.] A search that reports the best $k$ moves with their scores instead of one.
  \item[Lock convoy.] A collapse of throughput in which threads queue repeatedly on one contended lock.
  \item[HalfKAv2\_hm, FullThreats.] Names of input feature sets of Stockfish's NNUE networks.
  \item[Transformer-enabled fraction.] The share of the hybrid's moves on which the time-safety rule leaves the transformer search on.
  \item[Adjudication, time forfeit.] A game ended by the match manager's draw, resignation or move-cap rule; a game lost by exceeding the clock.
  \item[Syzygy tablebases.] Exact databases of endgame positions with few pieces, used here only by the oracle judges.
  \item[Root hints and verification.] Moves passed from the MCTS root to the alpha-beta root at the start of a move; they set the order of alpha-beta's first iteration, and a short restricted alpha-beta search checks the first five of them.
  \item[Veto.] The arbiter's rule that blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks it clearly below its own move; most override predicates can lift it (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}).
\end{description}

\section{The arbiter as frozen}
\label{app:ladder}
This appendix lists the decision rules of the frozen engine (source commit \texttt{365f36a}, \texttt{src/hybrid/hybrid\_search.cpp}) under the settings of the hybrid arms. Notation: $R$ is the number of visits the MCTS root received in this move (visits reused from the previous move's tree excluded) and $R_{\mathrm{tot}}$ the number including them; $B$ is the visits of the MCTS move in this move and $S=B/R$; $G$ is the value gap between the MCTS move and the best other move at the root; $c=300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$ is the MCTS move's score in centipawns and $\Delta=c-s_{\mathrm{AB}}$, where $s_{\mathrm{AB}}$ is alpha-beta's score; $Q_g$ is the value gap between the MCTS move and alpha-beta's move in the MCTS statistics. \emph{Sane} means the visit counts are consistent with the playouts and evaluations; \emph{strong} means $R\ge 180$, $B\ge 72$, $S\ge 0.24$, and $B\ge 512$ or $S\ge 0.55$; \emph{clear} means alpha-beta completed depth 10 with $|s_{\mathrm{AB}}|\ge 15$. Every rule also requires a clock-managed search with a positive budget, which holds in all the matches (several names say ``fixed budget'' for historical reasons). Alpha-beta's root scores are in Stockfish's internal units (about three per centipawn) while $s_{\mathrm{AB}}$ and $\Delta$ are in centipawns, so the thresholds mix units.
