\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The strongest chess programs follow one of two designs. One is alpha-beta search with an efficiently updatable neural-network evaluation (NNUE), exemplified by Stockfish~\cite{stockfish,nasu2018,sf12}, which searches tens of millions of nodes per second on a CPU. The other is Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) guided by a deep policy--value network, the approach of AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018} and of Leela Chess Zero (Lc0)~\cite{lc0,monroe2026}, which evaluates far fewer positions (AlphaZero searched about 60{,}000 per second against Stockfish's 60 million~\cite{silver2018}) and compensates with the quality of each evaluation. On consumer systems-on-chip such as Apple's M-series~\cite{applem2} the CPU and the GPU share one system memory~\cite{appleunified}, which makes it cheap to run both searches on the same position at the same time and to arbitrate between their answers. Whether that arbitration adds playing strength to a Stockfish-class CPU search is an empirical question, and we know of no controlled measurement that answers it.

\ifanon An earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\else Our earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\fi{} against the engine's source code and the retained records showed that its central quantitative claims (the ratings, the speed-up and the throughput comparison with Lc0) do not hold: each rating was an average, over opponents, of an assumed opponent rating plus the Elo difference implied by a five-game score; the alpha-beta component is Stockfish's own search; and several implementation statements were wrong (Section~\ref{sec:chapter} lists the main ones\ifanon\else; a Correction has been prepared\fi). This paper is the controlled study the chapter lacked. It takes the engine, updates it as described in Section~\ref{sec:prep}, freezes it, and asks one pre-registered question with everything else held equal:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does running an Lc0-class transformer MCTS on the GPU concurrently with a Stockfish-class alpha-beta search on the CPU, with a final arbiter, change playing strength relative to the alpha-beta search alone, at equal wall-clock and equal CPU-thread budget on one machine?}
\end{quote}

Equal CPU-thread budget means the same \texttt{Threads} setting for both arms; the hybrid's single MCTS worker runs as one extra thread that mostly waits on the GPU (Section~\ref{sec:system}). The answer is measured as an Elo difference with a confidence interval, decomposed into the cost of the resources the GPU path consumes and the value it returns, and explained by what the two searches disagree about and who is right when they do. The study design was written down before the data were collected (Section~\ref{sec:method}); the engine was frozen at a tagged commit; every number is traced to a retained artifact, and every table and figure is generated from the artifacts by a script.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled, pre-registered methodology for evaluating hybrid or ensemble chess engines that share one machine: a fair-resource policy, pentanomial statistics with normalised Elo, unbalanced human openings, telemetry of when the GPU path is actually active, component isolation and scaling cells, with all artifacts released (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item The as-built \MF{} system on Apple Silicon: a concurrent alpha-beta plus MCTS runtime with shared root state, MCTS-to-alpha-beta root-ordering hints and a gated arbiter; the BT4 transformer on Metal Performance Shaders Graph; and measurements of its batch-1 latency ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:system}) and of the contention between the two searches inside games (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Measurements answering the research questions of Section~\ref{sec:rqs}: the marginal value of the GPU search at equal budget, the decomposition into thread cost and GPU value, the parity of the alpha-beta core with Stockfish 19, and the sensitivity to time control (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Alpha-beta engines}
Stockfish combines alpha-beta pruning~\cite{knuth1975} with aspiration windows, null-move and futility pruning, late-move reductions and a shared transposition table, and since 2020 it has evaluated positions with an NNUE network~\cite{nasu2018,sf12}, whose first layer is updated incrementally as moves are made and unmade. Its development is driven by Fishtest, a distributed testing framework whose statistical conventions (game pairs with colour swap, unbalanced opening books, pentanomial statistics, sequential tests) are designed to measure small strength differences~\cite{fishtest,stockfish,sfbooks}.

\subsection{Neural-network MCTS engines}
MCTS~\cite{coulom2006,kocsis2006,browne2012} with PUCT selection~\cite{rosin2011} and a policy--value network, as in AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018}, grows a search tree whose leaves are evaluated by the network, so its quality depends on how many evaluations fit into the time for a move. Lc0 is an open-source engine in the AlphaZero tradition whose current networks are transformers~\cite{monroe2026,lc0}; the BT4 network used here is one of them. Transformers have also been trained to play chess without explicit search~\cite{ruoss2024}. On a given GPU the evaluation rate is governed by batching: at small batch sizes, fixed framework overhead dominates inference time~\cite{fernandez2023}, and Section~\ref{sec:system} measures this for the backend used here. Running CPU and GPU work concurrently on one memory system has its own regimes of benefit and interference~\cite{mohite2026}.

\subsection{Combining minimax and MCTS}
The algorithmic literature on hybrids embeds one search inside the other. Baier and Winands embed shallow minimax searches in the selection, rollout and backpropagation phases of MCTS~\cite{baier2013,baier2015} and later add static state evaluations~\cite{baier2018}; Lanctot et al.\ back up an implicit minimax value alongside the Monte Carlo average~\cite{lanctot2014}; Huang shows that alpha-beta and MT-SSS* can be expressed as rollout algorithms~\cite{huang2015}, and Baier unifies MCTS and alpha-beta in one rollout-based algorithm~\cite{baier2017}. Ji and Thielscher vary the depth of the embedded minimax search dynamically~\cite{ji2023}. A common motivation for these hybrids is the weakness of sampling-based search in tactical positions: UCT can miss shallow traps that minimax finds~\cite{ramanujan2010}, and in synthetic chess-like games it can show lookahead pathology, although minimax is similarly susceptible in that setting~\cite{nguyen2024}. In the AlphaZero setting, Chen et al.\ add an alpha-beta phase to an AlphaZero-style player~\cite{chen2019}, Helfenstein et al.\ combine a mixture of experts with MCTS in chess~\cite{helfenstein2024}, and Cohen-Solal and Cazenave show that minimax-based learning remains competitive with MCTS-based learning~\cite{cohen2023}.

\subsection{Engines that combine or compare the paradigms}
Engine practice has its own precedents. Scorpio offers MCTS with alpha-beta rollouts and, with neural networks, a network-guided MCTS that uses alpha-beta searches as a prior~\cite{scorpio}; Komodo, since version 12, has an MCTS mode whose tree is expanded best-first from tiny alpha-beta searches instead of random playouts or a network~\cite{komodo}; a 2017 Stockfish branch experimented with a Monte Carlo search on top of Stockfish's evaluation~\cite{sfmcts2017}; the lc0-stockfish-hybrid project uses Stockfish as a filter inside Lc0's selection~\cite{lc0sfhybrid}; Ceres and Allie are independent MCTS searches on Lc0 networks~\cite{ceres,allie}; and Stoofvlees II, an MCTS engine whose design departs from the AlphaZero recipe, won two World Computer Chess Championships~\cite{pascutto2025}. The system studied here differs in that both searches run concurrently on the same position, exchanging only root-level hints, and a final arbiter chooses between their answers; the question of this paper is whether that arbitration is worth anything at a fixed hardware budget, and we know of no controlled measurement of it among the works above. Adjacent work models human play across skill levels with a neural policy~\cite{maia2} and combines such policies with engine search~\cite{carlson2026}, for goals other than playing strength.

\subsection{Measuring strength}
Elo differences between near-equal engines are small relative to game-to-game noise, and the measurement conventions matter as much as the games. Fishtest's methodology, documented by Van den Bergh~\cite{vdbergh_gsprt,vdbergh_rw,vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}, plays colour-swapped game pairs from unbalanced human openings (UHO)~\cite{sfbooks,pohl2024} so that draws do not swamp the signal, and reports pentanomial statistics and normalised Elo; the book choice itself is debated~\cite{uhocritique}. Draw modelling in Elo~\cite{szczecinski2020} and uncertainty estimation in paired-comparison experiments~\cite{glickman1999} are the statistical background; Bayesian Elo~\cite{bayeselo} and Ordo~\cite{ordo} fit static paired-comparison models to multi-engine results (Ordo is the tool used here), and whole-history rating~\cite{coulom2008} extends such models to players whose strength changes over time. Ratings from public lists such as CCRL are specific to their reference hardware and time control~\cite{ccrl} and cannot be imported to another machine; in its seventeenth season the Top Chess Engine Championship gave CPU and GPU engines separate lower leagues, each on its own hardware, before both met in the Premier Division~\cite{haworth2020}. Test-position suites, mostly tactical (the Strategic Test Suite is not)~\cite{bratko1982,sts,eret,arasan,lichesspuzzles}, measure something other than playing strength and, for an engine that was tuned on them, measure it optimistically.

\section{The system as built}
\label{sec:system}

\MF{} is a single UCI binary with three modes selected by options: plain alpha-beta (\texttt{AB}), stand-alone transformer MCTS, and the hybrid that runs both concurrently. This section describes the engine as frozen for this study (\ifanon source and binary in the archive~\depositcite{}; the binary was rebuilt once from the same source against an upgraded system library, as described in Section~\ref{sec:amend}\else repository tag \texttt{paper-v2-freeze-rc2}: the binary is built from the source of commit \texttt{365f36a} and was rebuilt once against an upgraded system library, as described in Section~\ref{sec:amend}\fi); Section~\ref{sec:chapter} lists where the earlier chapter's description differed. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the threads and data flows.

\subsection{Alpha-beta component}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish's search and NNUE evaluation. At the freeze it is a port of Stockfish 19 with its SFNNv16 network (\texttt{nn-1a298aa575a0.nnue}, 98.5 MB); the engine's \texttt{bench} command reproduces the upstream signature of 2{,}497{,}913 nodes, so the search is node-for-node Stockfish's. %src: results/paper_v2/queue_paper_log.txt (bench lines 25, 60, 98, 105); upstream tag sf_19; 03 §0
Its local changes are few: the root accepts move-ordering hints from the MCTS component, the previous principal variation is kept after an unproven fail-high, the hybrid can set the size of the thread pool, and there are an AArch64 spin-wait hint, two branch-likelihood hints, thread quality-of-service classes, a de-duplication of \texttt{searchmoves}, a transposition-table allocation check and a NEON kernel. There is no GPU code in the alpha-beta search; NNUE runs on the CPU by design, because incremental CPU evaluation is faster than any GPU round trip at the granularity of a node. On the test machine the search reaches 1.53 million nodes/s on one thread, 11.81 million on eight threads and 13.52 million on all twelve cores at fixed depth (Figure~\ref{fig:m2}; measured on 13 October 2026 with \ifanon a pre-freeze build\else build \texttt{8777b99}\fi{} whose search code is the frozen one; the re-run on the frozen binary follows the cells). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/ab_scaling.json (depth 16, median of 5)

\subsection{MCTS component and neural-network backend}
The MCTS component follows Lc0's search: PUCT selection with first-play urgency, a policy softmax temperature, tree reuse between moves and a direct-mapped cache of network outputs keyed on the position. Nodes are 128 bytes and aligned to the M-series cache line; a 4-byte \texttt{os\_unfair\_lock} protects score updates. The BT4 transformer (\texttt{BT4-1024x15x32h}, 15 encoder blocks, 32 heads, 1024-wide; 383 MB of weights) is built once as a Metal Performance Shaders graph~\cite{mpsgraph} with an FP16 body and FP32 inputs and outputs, and encoded into a command buffer for each evaluation; one evaluation is in flight at a time. The stand-alone MCTS mode runs one worker at batch size 1; the hybrid runs one worker.

Batch-1 inference is the structural ceiling of this design. On the M2 Max with no game running (13 October 2026, before host load was logged; \ifanon a pre-freeze build\else build \texttt{8777b99}\fi{} whose network code is the frozen one) a single BT4 evaluation takes 7.40 ms in FP16 (135 positions/s) and 8.74 ms in FP32; a batch of 8 takes 19.7 ms (407 positions/s) and a batch of 64 takes 119 ms (538 positions/s), so batching trades a fourfold throughput gain for proportionally staler tree statistics (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency.json (2026-10-13 sweep, queue_paper_log.txt; to be refreshed from nn_latency_frozen.json)
A search with Lc0 0.32.1's Metal backend and the same weights reaches 165 nodes/s at minibatch 1 and at best 725 nodes/s (minibatch 32) on the same machine. \MF{}'s evaluation rate is therefore below Lc0's search rate at batch size 1, the setting its search uses, and at each program's best batch size (538 against 725 per second); at batch sizes 2 to 8 it is above, at 16 and 32 below and at 64 about equal (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). The two tools count different things (network evaluations against search nodes), so these comparisons are indicative only. %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency.json (lc0 nps_steady_median)
Inside a game the MCTS worker competes with eight alpha-beta threads for memory bandwidth and scheduling; its observed rate is reported with the game telemetry in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Hybrid runtime and arbiter}
On every move the coordinator starts the alpha-beta search and the MCTS search on the same position and lets Stockfish's clock-based time manager own the move (\texttt{HybridFollowABTime}); the MCTS side is bounded by the coordinator's per-move budget. Alpha-beta keeps all $T$ threads and the single MCTS worker runs as an extra thread that mostly blocks on the GPU (\texttt{HybridOversubscribe}). The two searches publish their root state through two cache-line-aligned structures whose fields are written with relaxed atomic stores and made visible through a release-ordered flag and counter; the MCTS top-8 moves pre-order the alpha-beta root, and a sub-search of up to 240 ms (at most an eighth of the budget) verifies the top MCTS candidates when the per-move budget is at least one second. The final decision is alpha-beta's move unless one of a ladder of gated predicates selects the MCTS move (for example when MCTS has a large visit majority and alpha-beta does not reject the move in its own verification); the pawn-lever and quiet-attack tie-break overrides, which were added after the chapter, are off. A time-safety rule disables the transformer for a move when the estimated budget is below 400 ms or the remaining clock below 3 s; the move is then plain alpha-beta. The position classifier that the chapter described as weighting the decision only lengthens the budget and sets a node minimum. The agreement-based early stop is gated on at least 100 MCTS playouts and fires rarely (on 3 of the 3{,}278 moves with the transformer active in the 30+0.3 pilot); its time saving has not been measured. %src: results/paper_v2/RQ1a_pilot/engines.log.gz ("engines agree, stopping early"), moves.jsonl

The study started from the engine as of July 2026, which already differed from the version the chapter described (May 2026): for example, the transformer's low-time fallback was then a fixed 5~s, and the tie-break overrides did not exist yet. The alpha-beta core was moved from a January 2026 Stockfish development snapshot to Stockfish 19 (+\elo{62.0}{28.2} over the old base at 10+0.1, 136 games); %src: 08 §A sf19port_vs_preport_ab_10p01_v2

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Principles and fair-resource policy}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish, so its strength is not a contribution and is never compared across machines. Every primary comparison is \emph{same binary, same machine, same wall-clock, same total CPU-thread budget}, with the GPU search switched on or off; the thread budget is the \texttt{Threads} setting, and the hybrid runs its MCTS worker as one extra, mostly GPU-blocked thread (amendment~3). The protocol~\depositcite{} fixed the hypotheses, cells, sample sizes, statistics and decision rules before data collection and was amended only by numbered entries written before the affected cell was interpreted (eleven amendments, the last a disclosure; Section~\ref{sec:amend}). All games, the engine logs of the cells with a hybrid arm, traces, manifests with hashes and seeds, and thermal logs are retained, and every figure and table is generated from them by a script.

\subsection{Environment}
All measurements were taken on one Apple M2 Max (8 performance and 4 efficiency CPU cores, 38-core GPU, 32 GB unified memory) running macOS 27.2 and prevented from sleeping. The protocol assumed an otherwise idle machine; the host was in fact shared with other work, so the CPU used by other processes was logged every minute and games were scheduled around it (amendments 8--10 in Section~\ref{sec:amend}). Games were managed by fastchess v1.8.2-alpha at concurrency 1. Reference engines were Stockfish 19 (tag \texttt{sf\_19}, PGO build) and Lc0 0.32.1 with the Metal backend and the same BT4 weights. Syzygy 3--4--5 tablebases were available only to the oracle judges of Section~\ref{sec:oracle}, never to the engines under test.

\subsection{Match protocol}
\label{sec:match}
Each cell is a fixed number of game pairs: every opening is played twice with colours swapped. Openings are drawn in a seeded random order from Fishtest's \texttt{UHO\_Lichess\_4852\_v1} book of 2{,}632{,}036 unbalanced human positions~\cite{sfbooks}; the same seed is used in every cell of a research question, so arms and cells see the same opening sample. Engines are restarted for every game and do not ponder; the \MF{} arms use a 50 ms move overhead (the protocol's setting after a 12 ms overrun at 10+0.1 with the 10 ms default), and the reference engines keep their defaults (10 ms for Stockfish 19). Games are adjudicated as drawn after move 34 when both engines' scores have stayed within $\pm 20$ cp for eight consecutive moves each (Fishtest's draw rule) and as lost after three consecutive scores beyond $\pm 600$ cp from both engines' perspectives, and they are capped at 200 moves; adjudicated games, time forfeits and crashes are counted and reported per cell, and no game is excluded after the fact. Each cell is split into four blocks of equal size that draw disjoint openings; the blocks of the four primary cells are interleaved round-robin over the calendar so that thermal and background drift are balanced across them, and later cells run block after block; the thermal state is logged every minute. The hybrid's telemetry (\texttt{HybridTrace}) records, for every move, whether the transformer was active, the MCTS playouts and evaluations, both candidates, and the arbiter's decision and reason.

\subsection{Statistics}
The primary estimator is the pentanomial model: each game pair scores 0, $\tfrac12$, 1, $1\tfrac12$ or 2 points, and the mean and variance of the pair score give the logistic Elo difference with a 95\% normal-approximation confidence interval, the likelihood of superiority (LOS), and normalised Elo (nElo), which divides the score excess by its per-game standard deviation, so that the number of games needed to resolve a difference does not depend on the draw rate or the opening book~\cite{vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}. The statistics are computed independently from the PGN by our own script and must agree with fastchess's report to rounding. Multi-engine tables use Ordo~\cite{ordo} anchored to Stockfish 19 at 0 on this hardware; no absolute rating is claimed. The oracle study uses paired bootstrap intervals over positions (10{,}000 resamples). RQ1a at 30+0.3 is the single confirmatory test; all other cells are estimates with confidence intervals and are labelled exploratory, no family-wise correction is applied, and every planned cell is reported whether or not it ran to completion.

\subsection{Research questions and cells}
\label{sec:rqs}
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (confirmatory).] Does the GPU search change strength at equal budget? Hybrid(8) vs AB(8) at 30 s + 0.3 s per game, 1{,}000 pairs (RQ1a); 60 s + 0.6 s, 500 pairs (RQ1b, the regime with the most GPU participation); the decision rule is the sign of the 95\% interval of RQ1a.
  \item[RQ2 (isolation).] What do two threads cost and what does the GPU search return given six threads? AB(6) vs AB(8) (RQ2a) and Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(6) (RQ2b), 500 pairs each at 30+0.3; Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(8) (RQ2c) is their combination.
  \item[RQ5 (component checks).] Is the alpha-beta core at parity with Stockfish 19 (AB(8) vs SF19(8), 500 pairs; RQ5c), and is the MCTS implementation at parity with Lc0 at matched settings (MCTS(1) vs Lc0-mb1, 300 pairs; RQ5a)?
  \item[RQ6 (arbitration).] From the traces: disagreement rate, visits at decision time, decision reasons, and how often the MCTS move is chosen (RQ6a). An oracle study on 600 disagreement positions stratified by game phase and MCTS visit count judges both candidates with Stockfish 19 (8 threads, 60 s, both moves scored at equal depth with multi-PV, tablebases) and Lc0 (30{,}000 nodes), reports the fraction where each side is better and the expected-score loss of each move, and bounds what any arbiter could gain (RQ6b). An ablation with the MCTS-to-alpha-beta hints and the verification sub-search off (RQ6d) measures the hint channel.
  \item[Microbenchmarks.] BT4 latency and throughput against batch size for \MF{} and Lc0 (M1), alpha-beta nodes/s against threads (M2), and, from the game traces, in-game MCTS evaluations per second, GPU-active fraction and playouts per move against time control (M3 and M5); M4, the memory footprint, is in Section~\ref{sec:prep}.
\end{description}

\subsection{Pilot, freeze and amendments}
\label{sec:amend}
A 50-pair pilot of RQ1a preceded the freeze and checked the runner, the statistics against fastchess, forfeits (none), the draw rate and the GPU-active fraction. The pilot found the memory defect described in Section~\ref{sec:prep}, which was fixed before the freeze; the pilot games are reported as a diagnostic and are not part of RQ1a. Eleven amendments were recorded, each before the affected cell was interpreted: (1) the hybrid arm is the shipped configuration after the time-management and tie-break changes; (2) engines restart per game; (3) the shipped hybrid is oversubscribed, so the split configuration became the ablation of RQ2; (4) the GPU-active fraction at 30+0.3 (about 50\% of hybrid moves) fell short of the 80\% feasibility target, which was kept as a covariate and led to RQ1b being promoted; (5) RQ6d was promoted after the pilot; (6) the strata of the oracle study were defined; (7) a system-library upgrade on the host broke the frozen binary during the first block of RQ1a, so the same source was rebuilt, re-verified (bench signature, unit suite, network reference outputs; the batch-parity check matched up to batch 8, and at batch 16 one FP16 policy logit differed by 0.0056 against a tolerance of 0.005, a batch size the engine does not use) and re-frozen with its dynamic libraries bundled, and RQ1a restarted from its first opening with the 183 earlier games set aside; (8) because the host was shared with other work, blocks are played in chunks that start only when the CPU used by other processes, averaged over five minutes, is below three cores (the engines need about nine of the twelve; block 0 of RQ1a and RQ2a had been played before the gate existed), and every cell is also reported with the pairs that overlapped a minute above three cores removed (Section~\ref{sec:threats}); (9) after four time forfeits during a load spike inside a chunk, chunks were shortened from ten pairs to four and a watchdog stops a running chunk at the next completed pair when the two-minute external load exceeds 4.5 cores; (10) after a further forfeit during a spike below that trigger, the watchdog uses the gate's threshold of three cores, and time forfeits are reported per arm (Table~\ref{tab:health}); (11) an interim reading of each cell has been recorded after every block for monitoring, beyond the running fastchess printout the protocol allowed, with no decision rule attached (this amendment discloses the practice; amendments 8--10 were written after the first reading). The measurements were interrupted by host reboots, including kernel panics, and paused whenever the gate was closed; every block resumed from the next unused opening of the same seeded order, and the manifests record every invocation of every block. No game was deleted. A game whose colour-swapped partner was not played enters no pair and its opening is replayed, except once: before a bookkeeping fix, a watchdog stop split a pair and its opening was skipped, so RQ1a has one pair fewer than planned.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Single platform.} Every measurement comes from one M2 Max; the direction of the scaling cells is informative but the numbers are not portable, and the CUDA build of the same engine is out of scope. \textbf{Opening book.} UHO openings make differences measurable but exaggerate Elo spreads relative to balanced books; nElo is reported for that reason and the book critique applies~\cite{uhocritique}. \textbf{GPU-active fraction.} The shipped time-safety rule is part of the system under test; at 30+0.3 the transformer is active on about half of the hybrid's moves, and at 10+0.1 it would be active on almost none because the move budget is mostly below 400 ms, so results at short time controls are largely alpha-beta results, and the fraction is reported as a covariate. \textbf{Tuning leakage.} The hybrid and MCTS heuristics were tuned on Bratko--Kopec and Lichess puzzle samples; any suite result for \MF{} is optimistic and is labelled so; game results are unaffected. \textbf{Reference drift.} Stockfish 19 is the alpha-beta base and the parity reference; the gap to the chapter-era base is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:prep}. \textbf{Adjudication.} The draw and resignation rules end a game only when both engines' own scores agree that it is decided; the 200-move cap ends a game regardless of the scores, and capped games are included in the adjudication counts. The protocol's re-run of one RQ1a block without adjudication has not been run for this version. \textbf{Move overhead.} The \MF{} arms run with a 50 ms move overhead and Stockfish 19 with its 10 ms default, so in RQ5c the \MF{} arm has 40 ms less per move; this favours Stockfish 19 slightly and is part of that cell as pre-registered. \textbf{Shared host.} The machine was not idle. Over the \HostLogSamples{} minutes logged from \HostLogFirst{} to \HostLogLast{}, other processes used a median of \HostLogMedianCores{} cores and more than three cores in \HostLogOverPct\% of minutes; over the \HostGameSamples{} minutes that overlapped a game, the median was \HostGameMedianCores{} cores (95th percentile \HostGamePninetyfiveCores{}) and \HostGameOverPct\% of those minutes exceeded three cores (the gate checks the load only before a chunk starts, and until amendment~10 the watchdog stopped a chunk only above 4.5 cores). Because the arms of RQ1a and RQ2 need different numbers of free cores, load can bias as well as blur them: time forfeits came in minutes of high outside load wherever the load was logged, and fell mostly on the alpha-beta arm, which favours the hybrid in RQ1a (amendment~10; Table~\ref{tab:health}); every cell is therefore reported with and without the pairs that overlapped a minute of more than three cores of outside load (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). \textbf{Interruptions.} RQ1a was restarted once on a rebuilt binary, and the cells were paused and resumed many times by the load gate and by host reboots (Section~\ref{sec:amend}); per-block results are reported so that any effect of the gaps is visible. \textbf{Statistics.} One confirmatory test at fixed $N$; everything else is exploratory and reported in full.
