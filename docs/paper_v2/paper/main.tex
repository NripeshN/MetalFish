\begin{abstract}
Can a GPU make a Stockfish-class chess engine stronger? We test one configuration of the question.
\MF{} runs a transformer Monte Carlo tree search (one worker evaluating Lc0's BT4 network at batch
size~1) on the GPU of an Apple M2 Max beside Stockfish~19's alpha-beta search on its CPU, and an arbiter
chooses between their moves. Under a protocol published before the first confirmatory game, the shipped
hybrid played 2{,}002 games against the same binary's alpha-beta search at the same thread setting (30~s
plus 0.3~s per move, unbalanced openings in colour-swapped pairs). It scored $-4.3$~Elo (95\% CI
$-10.7$ to $+2.0$): inconclusive at this sample size, with gains above 2.0~Elo excluded. The treatment
is the hybrid runtime as a whole, so the result cannot separate what the GPU search adds from what the
machinery around it costs, and a null result would not show that a better-driven GPU search cannot
help. The arbiter played the MCTS move on 7 of 25{,}138 disagreements. In exploratory cells the hybrid
lost 40.5~Elo at 60+0.6 (95\% CI $-49.6$ to $-31.4$), and a split configuration that ran alpha-beta on
six of the eight threads while the transformer was enabled lost 17.0~Elo to six-thread alpha-beta. On
GitHub-hosted virtual M1 machines with three cores, analysed only against each other, the hybrid lost
35.9~Elo at 30+0.3 and 16.7~Elo at 10+0.1, where every move it played was alpha-beta's; switching off
the MCTS search's hints and verification sub-search changed the 30+0.3 result by $+9.8 \pm 14.8$~Elo,
no detectable effect. Against Stockfish~19 the alpha-beta core scored $+1.4$~Elo ($-7.3$ to $+10.1$),
no difference detected. On a runner, \MF{}'s complete MCTS stack scored $+47.2$~Elo ($+26.0$ to
$+68.7$) against Lc0 at one thread and minibatch~1, a time-sensitive comparison of two search-plus-backend
stacks, not of the searches alone. \pending{oracle study RQ6b-R: one sentence with its bound} The ratings
reported in an earlier conference chapter on the system do not hold; a Correction has been prepared for
its publisher. All games, traces, logs and scripts are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The strongest chess programs follow one of two designs. One is alpha-beta search with an efficiently updatable neural-network evaluation (NNUE), exemplified by Stockfish~\cite{stockfish,nasu2018,sf12}, which searches tens of millions of nodes per second on a CPU. The other is Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) guided by a deep policy--value network, the approach of AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018} and of Leela Chess Zero (Lc0)~\cite{lc0,monroe2026}, which evaluates far fewer positions (AlphaZero searched about 60{,}000 per second against Stockfish's 60 million~\cite{silver2018}) and compensates with the quality of each evaluation. Both searches can run on the same position at the same time, with an arbiter between their answers, on any machine with a CPU and a GPU; on systems-on-chip such as Apple's M-series~\cite{applem2}, where the CPU and the GPU share one memory~\cite{appleunified}, this is compact, and the two searches compete for shared resources. Whether that adds playing strength to a Stockfish-class search is an instance of a general question, how to split test-time compute between a fast search with a cheap evaluation (here more than ten million positions per second on eight cores) and a slower learned evaluator (here at most 139 positions per second at batch size~1); we know of no controlled measurement of it in chess.

\ifanon An earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\else Our earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\fi{} against the engine's source and the retained records showed that its central quantitative claims do not hold: each rating was an average, over opponents, of an assumed opponent rating plus the Elo difference implied by a five-game score; the alpha-beta component is Stockfish's own search; and several implementation statements were wrong (Section~\ref{sec:chapter}\ifanon\else; a Correction has been prepared for the publisher and not yet filed\fi). This paper is the controlled study the chapter lacked. It updates the engine (Section~\ref{sec:prep}), freezes it, and asks one pre-registered question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does running an Lc0-class transformer MCTS (one worker, batch size~1) on the GPU of an Apple M2 Max concurrently with a Stockfish-class alpha-beta search on its CPU, with a final arbiter that chooses between their root moves, change playing strength relative to the alpha-beta search alone, at equal wall-clock and equal CPU-thread budget?}
\end{quote}

Equal budget means the same \texttt{Threads} setting for both arms; the hybrid's MCTS worker is an extra thread that waits on the GPU during each evaluation. ``Lc0-class'' refers to the network, not to Lc0's search configuration, from which \MF{}'s MCTS also departs (Section~\ref{sec:system}). The treatment is the shipped hybrid runtime as a whole, which can change the outcome through five channels: the MCTS root hints and the verification sub-search (in RQ1a the hints were present on 84\% of the transformer-enabled moves and the verification ran on 18\%); the agreement rule, which can end a move early; contention between the two searches for cores, memory and the clock; the costs of the machinery that arise even when no GPU search runs; and the arbiter's override, which never selected the MCTS move in the frozen build's gate L14 (0 of 1{,}344 disagreements) or in the pilot (0 of 1{,}165). RQ1a cannot separate these channels; whether arbitration could be worth anything is the question of an oracle study (RQ6b-R) that judges the disagreements with two engines. %src: results/paper_v2/sprt/L14_hybrid_lean_vs_ab8_30p03/moves.jsonl; results/paper_v2/RQ1a_pilot/moves.jsonl
The protocol was merged into the public repository before the first confirmatory game, every amendment carries its merge time (Section~\ref{sec:amend}), the engine was frozen at a tagged commit, and every number and figure is generated from retained artifacts by a script.

\paragraph{Results.} Over 2{,}002 games at 30+0.3 (999 colour-swapped pairs and four games without their partner), the shipped hybrid scored $-4.3$~Elo against AB(8), with a 95\% interval of $[-10.7, +2.0]$: by the registered rule inconclusive at this $N$, with no detectable effect at the achieved resolution of $\pm 6.3$~Elo. What this outcome means was fixed by amendment~12, merged on 27 October about four hours after an interim reading of RQ1a's first two blocks ($-9.4 \pm 9.1$) had been committed and before the cell was interpreted on 31 October; the amendment's reading, that the GPU path does not pay for itself at this budget, is a clause added after that interim, and what the data support is that gains above 2.0~Elo are excluded. The transformer was enabled on 53\% of the hybrid's moves, and the arbiter played the MCTS move on 7 of the 25{,}138 moves on which the two searches disagreed. In the earlier split configuration, two of the eight alpha-beta threads were worth 19.8~Elo to plain alpha-beta (24.7 at zero outside load), and the split hybrid lost a further 17.0~Elo against alpha-beta on six threads with at least as many alpha-beta threads as its opponent on every move. At 60+0.6 (RQ1b, exploratory) the hybrid scored $-40.5$~Elo, $[-49.6, -31.4]$, and the agreement rule ended 48.6\% of the transformer-enabled moves early (1.0\% at 30+0.3); RQ1a and RQ1b differ in time control as well as in GPU participation.

The measurements not yet played when RQ1b finished moved to GitHub-hosted virtual M1 machines with three cores (amendment~15; Section~\ref{sec:runners}); these cells are exploratory and compared only with each other. There the shipped hybrid lost to alpha-beta at the same thread setting by 35.9~Elo at 30+0.3 ($[-46.2, -25.6]$; RQ1a-R) and by 16.7~Elo at 10+0.1 ($[-24.4, -9.0]$; RQ1c-R), where every move it played was alpha-beta's: on such a machine the machinery costs strength even when it does not change the move. Switching off the hints and the verification sub-search changed the 30+0.3 result by $+9.8 \pm 14.8$~Elo (RQ6d-R against RQ1a-R), no detectable effect. \pending{the oracle study RQ6b-R: one sentence}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled methodology, fixed in advance in a time-stamped public protocol, for evaluating hybrid or ensemble chess engines that share one machine: a fair-resource policy, telemetry of when the GPU path is enabled, host-load logging with a scheduling gate, isolation cells and an oracle analysis with its reading rules fixed before it was read, combined with Fishtest's conventions (colour-swapped pairs, unbalanced openings, pentanomial statistics), with all artifacts released (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A corrected as-built description of \MF{}~\cite{niketan2026chapter}, including where its MCTS departs from Lc0's, with the measured batch-1 ceiling of its GPU evaluator and the in-game evaluation and node rates, and how they vary with the cores alpha-beta leaves free (Sections~\ref{sec:system} and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Measurements of the shipped hybrid runtime against alpha-beta at an equal \texttt{Threads} setting, at two time controls on the M2 Max and two on three-core runners; of the hints-and-verification channel, on the runners; of the split configuration as built against alpha-beta on the threads it keeps; and of the alpha-beta core's parity with Stockfish~19 as a validity condition (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item The design of an oracle analysis that bounds, under two stated judges, what any arbiter choosing between the two root candidates at decision time could gain, with the arbitration telemetry of every hybrid move (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}), and \pending{the oracle study's results on the runners (RQ6b-R)}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The two paradigms}
Stockfish combines alpha-beta pruning~\cite{knuth1975} with aspiration windows, null-move and futility pruning, late-move reductions and a shared transposition table, and since 2020 it has evaluated positions with an NNUE network~\cite{nasu2018,sf12}, whose first layer is updated incrementally as moves are made and unmade. MCTS~\cite{coulom2006,kocsis2006,browne2012} with PUCT selection~\cite{rosin2011} and a policy--value network, as in AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018}, grows a tree whose leaves the network evaluates, so its quality depends on how many evaluations fit into the time for a move. Lc0 is an open-source engine in this tradition whose current networks are transformers~\cite{monroe2026,lc0}, among them the BT4 network used here; transformers have also been trained to play without explicit search~\cite{ruoss2024}. On a GPU the evaluation rate is governed by batching: at small batch sizes fixed framework overhead dominates~\cite{fernandez2023}, and batched MCTS keeps its statistics usable with a virtual loss~\cite{chaslot2008} or by correcting selection for the simulations in flight~\cite{liu2020wuuct}. Co-executing one workload on a unified-memory Apple chip's CPU and GPU helps in some phases of language-model inference and not in others~\cite{mohite2026}.  The paradigms are not independent in what they know: Stockfish's networks have been trained partly on positions evaluated by Leela Chess Zero since Stockfish~14~\cite{sf14}, and all of Stockfish~19's training positions were rescored with a strong Leela network~\cite{stockfish}. A transformer consulted a few dozen times per move may therefore add less independent information to Stockfish~19 than the diversity results below suggest; this redundancy and the under-provisioning of the GPU side (Section~\ref{sec:system}) predict different outcomes for a GPU side driven harder, and this study does not distinguish them.

\subsection{Combining minimax and MCTS}
Algorithmic hybrids embed one search inside the other: shallow minimax searches in the phases of MCTS~\cite{baier2013,baier2015,baier2018}, an implicit minimax value backed up beside the Monte Carlo average~\cite{lanctot2014}, rollout formulations of alpha-beta~\cite{huang2015,baier2017}, and minimax depth varied dynamically~\cite{ji2023}. A common motivation is the weakness of sampling-based search in tactical positions, where UCT can miss shallow traps that minimax finds~\cite{ramanujan2010}, although in synthetic games minimax is similarly susceptible to lookahead pathology~\cite{nguyen2024}. In the AlphaZero setting, an alpha-beta phase has been added to an AlphaZero-style player~\cite{chen2019}, a mixture of experts combined with MCTS in chess~\cite{helfenstein2024}, and minimax-based learning shown to remain competitive~\cite{cohen2023}. Engine practice has its own precedents: Scorpio's MCTS with alpha-beta rollouts or alpha-beta priors~\cite{scorpio}; Komodo's MCTS mode, expanded from tiny alpha-beta searches~\cite{komodo}; a 2017 Stockfish branch with a Monte Carlo search on Stockfish's evaluation~\cite{sfmcts2017}; the lc0-stockfish-hybrid project, which uses Stockfish as a filter inside Lc0's selection~\cite{lc0sfhybrid}; Ceres and Allie, independent MCTS searches on Lc0 networks~\cite{ceres,allie}; Stoofvlees II, an MCTS engine that departs from the AlphaZero recipe and won the 2023 World Computer Chess Championship~\cite{pascutto2025}; and ShashChess's experimental MCTS option for selected position types~\cite{shashchess}. 

\subsection{Choosing among several programs' moves}
Selecting a move from several programs' proposals predates neural networks. Alth\"ofer's multiple-choice systems give a human decider the candidate moves of two or more programs~\cite{althofer2003,althofer2004}; Clever~\&~Smart, an automatic version in which two versions of Shredder passed their proposals and search data to a control program that chose the move and managed the clock, finished second at the 1998 International Paderborn Computer Chess Championship~\cite{cleversmart}. In computer shogi, consultation by majority vote~\cite{obata2011} and an optimistic rule that plays the move of the program reporting the best evaluation~\cite{sugiyama2011} were compared in controlled matches, and consultation was used in the distributed system Akara 2010~\cite{hoki2013}. In computer Go, a team of diverse, individually weaker programs can outplay a uniform team of stronger ones~\cite{marcolino2013,marcolino2014}, which suggests that the positions where searches of different paradigms disagree are where an arbiter could gain. The hybrid studied here is a two-member consultation run concurrently on one chip under an equal \texttt{Threads} setting; with two members a vote cannot break a disagreement, and the arbiter defaults to the alpha-beta move unless gated predicates favour the MCTS move, which is closer to a veto rule than to the optimistic rule. What is new is not the consultation but a controlled, equal-budget measurement of the whole runtime, including what the consulting search does through the alpha-beta search it accompanies, together with the design of a judge-based bound on what any such arbiter could gain.

\subsection{Measuring strength}
Elo differences between near-equal engines are small relative to game-to-game noise, and the conventions matter as much as the games. Fishtest's methodology~\cite{vdbergh_gsprt,vdbergh_rw,vdbergh_nelo,fishtest} plays colour-swapped game pairs from unbalanced human openings (UHO)~\cite{sfbooks,pohl2024} so that draws do not swamp the signal, and reports pentanomial statistics and normalised Elo; the book choice itself is debated~\cite{uhocritique}. Draw modelling~\cite{szczecinski2020} and uncertainty in paired comparisons~\cite{glickman1999} are the statistical background; Bayesian Elo~\cite{bayeselo}, Ordo~\cite{ordo} and whole-history rating~\cite{coulom2008} fit paired-comparison models to multi-engine results. Ratings from public lists are specific to their reference hardware and time control~\cite{ccrl} and cannot be imported to another machine; in its seventeenth season the Top Chess Engine Championship gave CPU and GPU engines separate lower leagues on their own hardware~\cite{haworth2020}.

\section{The system as built}
\label{sec:system}

\MF{} is a single UCI binary with three modes selected by options: plain alpha-beta (\texttt{AB}), stand-alone transformer MCTS, and the hybrid that runs both concurrently. This section describes the engine as frozen for this study (\ifanon source and binary in the archive~\depositcite{}\else repository \url{https://github.com/NripeshN/MetalFish}, tag \texttt{paper-v2-freeze-rc2} at commit \texttt{8c0661d}, built from the source of commit \texttt{365f36a}\fi; the binary was rebuilt once against an upgraded system library, Section~\ref{sec:amend}); Section~\ref{sec:chapter} lists where the earlier chapter's description differed. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the threads and data flows.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{hybrid-architecture.pdf}
\caption{The hybrid runtime as frozen. The coordinator starts the Stockfish-derived alpha-beta search on all $T$ CPU threads and one MCTS worker that blocks on batch-1 GPU inference (\texttt{HybridOversubscribe}); both publish their root state, which the coordinator reads to choose the final move; the MCTS top moves are passed to the alpha-beta root as ordering hints and are verified by a short alpha-beta sub-search; the arbiter plays alpha-beta's move unless one of the gated override predicates fires. \subsection{Alpha-beta component}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish's search and NNUE evaluation: a port of Stockfish 19 with its SFNNv16 network (\texttt{nn-1a298aa575a0.nnue}), whose \texttt{bench} command reproduces the upstream signature of 2{,}497{,}913 nodes, so the search is node-for-node Stockfish's. %src: results/paper_v2/queue_paper_log.txt (bench lines 25, 60, 98, 105); upstream tag sf_19; 03 §0
Its local changes are few: the root accepts move-ordering hints from the MCTS component, the hybrid can set the size of the thread pool, and there are thread quality-of-service classes and small hints and checks. NNUE runs on the CPU by design. On the test machine the frozen binary searches 1.47 million nodes/s on one thread, 12.8 million on eight and 15.1 million on all twelve cores at fixed depth (Figure~\ref{fig:m2}). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/ab_scaling_frozen.json (depth 16, median of 5; 08 §B M2 frozen build)

\subsection{MCTS component and neural-network backend}
The MCTS component is a modified Lc0-style search. It reads Lc0's network format and uses PUCT selection with Lc0's constants (cpuct 1.745, base 38{,}739, factor 3.894, first-play-urgency reduction 0.33), Lc0's policy softmax temperature of 1.359 below the root, tree reuse between moves and a cache of network outputs keyed on the position. It departs from Lc0 0.32.1's defaults at the root in both modes: a root policy temperature of 1.6 instead of Lc0's single 1.359, and a root tactical capture probe that can redirect selection to particular captures when the root has 16 to 600 visits. The stand-alone mode, MCTS(1) of RQ5a-R, departs further: a root cpuct of 2.4, a smart-pruning factor of 0.5 instead of 1.33, a KLD-gain stopping rule that Lc0 has off by default, and three root selection heuristics; the hybrid switches those heuristics and the KLD rule off (Appendix~\ref{app:mcts}). Tuned on tactical test positions, these heuristics shape the MCTS candidate at the few dozen playouts per move the shipped worker reaches in games. Nodes are 128 bytes; the BT4 transformer (\texttt{BT4-1024x15x32h}, 15 encoder blocks, 32 heads, 1024-wide; 383 MB of weights) is built once as a Metal Performance Shaders graph~\cite{mpsgraph} with an FP16 body and FP32 inputs and outputs, and one evaluation is in flight at a time. %src: 365f36a src/mcts/search_params.h 25-107, src/uci/engine.cpp 191-222, src/uci/uci.cpp 1040-1090, 1139-1153, 1247-1270; Lc0 v0.32.1 src/neural/shared_params.cc:69, src/search/classic/params.cc 543-565, src/search/classic/stoppers/common.cc 89-92

Batch-1 inference is the structural ceiling of this design. With the frozen binary (1 November 2026, with outside processes using 1.5 to 3.6 cores during the run), a single BT4 evaluation takes 7.21~ms in FP16 (139 positions/s), the precision the engine uses, and 8.56~ms in FP32 (medians of 40 timed iterations after five warm-up iterations); a batch of 8 takes 18.9~ms (422 positions/s) and a batch of 64 takes 119~ms (538 positions/s), so batching could raise throughput almost fourfold (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). The engine as shipped does not batch; whether batching~\cite{chaslot2008,liu2020wuuct} would help it inside a game is not tested here. %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency_frozen.json (08 §B, M1 frozen build); src/uci/engine.cpp:140 (NNMetalFP16 true)
Lc0 0.32.1's search with its Metal backend and the same weights reached 217 nodes/s at minibatch 1 and 763 at minibatch 32 in the same run; its raw-evaluation benchmark failed on this machine (``Failed to import MPS module''). The two tools count different things, network evaluations and search nodes, so their rates are not compared here. %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency_frozen.json (lc0 nps_steady_median; 08 §B M1 frozen build)
Inside a game the MCTS worker shares the chip with eight alpha-beta threads. The halving of its in-game rate (Section~\ref{sec:results}) is consistent with competition for cores, the chip's system-level cache, memory bandwidth or its shared power and thermal budget; none of these was measured.

\subsection{Hybrid runtime and arbiter}
\label{sec:arbiter}
On every move the coordinator starts the alpha-beta search and the MCTS search on the same position. Alpha-beta keeps all $T$ threads and the MCTS worker runs as an extra thread (\texttt{HybridOversubscribe}). Stockfish's clock-based time manager sets alpha-beta's time (\texttt{HybridFollowABTime}), and the coordinator ends the move when the alpha-beta search returns, with two exceptions described below: the verification sub-search, spent before the time manager starts, and the agreement rule, which can stop alpha-beta early. The MCTS search is otherwise limited by the coordinator's own per-move budget (the remaining clock divided by 30 plus three quarters of the increment, within fixed bounds) less the move overhead. The searches publish their root state through cache-line-aligned structures read after both have stopped. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 468-500, 2849-2855, 4037-4042, 4124-4141; hybrid_search.h 36-141; L14 engines.log.gz ("subsearch stop grace expired": 0)

At the start of a move the alpha-beta side reads up to eight moves from the MCTS root (the subtree kept from the previous move, or nothing), which reorder its root moves for the first iteration only. When the coordinator's budget is at least one second, the first five hints are verified by a restricted alpha-beta search of $\min(240~\text{ms}, \text{budget}/8)$, with a further check of 80 to 160~ms for at most four quiet rook or queen checks when a queen is on the board. The verification shares the transposition and history tables with the main search, feeds two of the arbiter's predicates, is spent from the game clock before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move, and replaces state that the time manager carries from one search to the next. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 572-597, 3102-3176, 3537-3669, 3829-3864; search/search.cpp 59-98, 275-277

When both searches name the same move, it is played. When they disagree, the arbiter plays alpha-beta's move unless one of 36 predicates, tested in a fixed order, selects the MCTS move; 31 can fire under the tested settings (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}). A veto blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks its own move first and has searched the MCTS move and found it worse by a margin, or barely searched it; 24 of the 31 predicates can override the veto. The veto assumes that a move alpha-beta has searched to depth 10 and found worse is more often a tactical oversight of the shallow MCTS search than a judgement alpha-beta misses. Most predicates need at least 50 visits in the current move, many 100 to 300 and two 700 or 5{,}000, while the shipped worker reached a median of 38 playouts per transformer-enabled move in RQ1a; most of the ladder is therefore out of reach at 30+0.3 by construction, and the rarity of MCTS moves reflects the thresholds as much as the MCTS candidates' quality, which is what the oracle study is designed to judge (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}). The MCTS score that the predicates compare with alpha-beta's is $300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$, a conversion of the MCTS value calibrated to neither Lc0's nor Stockfish's centipawn scale.

A time-safety rule disables the transformer for a move when the estimated budget is below 400~ms or the remaining clock below 3~s; the move is then plain alpha-beta on the full thread pool, and the engine logs the rule but writes no decision trace. An agreement rule ends a move early when both searches have named the same move in three consecutive polls after a quarter of the coordinator's budget, with alpha-beta at depth 10 and at least 100 MCTS nodes (150 in positions the position classifier rates as highly complex); the classifier can only lengthen the coordinator's budget, but on the moves this rule stops it changes when alpha-beta is stopped. The rule ended 660 of the 68{,}456 transformer-enabled moves of RQ1a early (1.0\%) and 6{,}485 of the 38{,}645 of the split hybrid in RQ2b (16.8\%); its time saving was not measured directly. %src: results/paper_v2/{RQ1a,RQ2b}/engines.log ("engines agree, stopping early"; tables/flags.md); 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 1860-1873, 4058-4082, 4946-4951, 5288-5306, 5366-5480; classifier.cpp
With \texttt{HybridTrace} on, as in every hybrid arm, the coordinator writes one decision line per move before \texttt{bestmove}; its cost counts towards the move's time. The network is loaded and warmed up before the first \texttt{readyok}, so the game clock does not pay for initialisation. The predicates and thresholds, with source references, are in the arbiter reference \ifanon in the archive~\depositcite{}\else in the repository (\texttt{docs/paper\_v2/arbiter\_reference.md})\fi. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 4147-4214, 5483-5590; uci.cpp 161-164, 1579-1622, 1727-1737; mcts/search.cpp 278-322

\subsection{Engine preparation and freeze}
\label{sec:prep}
Before the freeze, an exploratory engineering programme (reported in the programme record~\depositcite{}) made four changes, each adopted on the evidence of a gating match. Here and below, $\pm$ is half the width of the 95\% pentanomial interval. The alpha-beta core was moved from a January 2026 Stockfish development snapshot to Stockfish 19 (+\elo{62.0}{28.2} over the old base at 10+0.1, 136 games); %src: 08 §A sf19port_vs_preport_ab_10p01_v2

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Principles and fair-resource policy}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish, so its strength is not a contribution and is never compared across machines. Every primary comparison is \emph{same binary, same machine, same wall-clock, same \texttt{Threads} setting}, with the hybrid runtime against plain alpha-beta. The \texttt{Threads} setting is a nominal budget: it equalises alpha-beta's threads and the clock, not the CPU time of the hybrid's extra MCTS worker (amendment~3), which was not measured, nor the GPU, memory and verification time the hybrid also uses. The protocol~\depositcite{} fixed the hypotheses, cells, sample sizes, statistics and decision rules before data collection: its frozen version (SHA-256 of the protocol file: \texttt{03020292ae3441df} \texttt{5a0eb2eab1e67403} \texttt{a0722a7ee85fcb59} \texttt{0dfac04e852a89f2}) was merged into the public repository on 14 October 2026 at 06:46~UTC\ifanon\else{} (pull request~\#77, a server-side time stamp)\fi, and the first confirmatory game started at 08:09~UTC. The registration is the author's own, in a public repository, not in a third-party registry, and it was amended only by numbered entries written before the affected cell was interpreted (Section~\ref{sec:amend}).

\subsection{Environment and configurations}
The workstation cells were played on one Apple M2 Max (8 performance and 4 efficiency CPU cores, 38-core GPU, 32 GB unified memory) running macOS 27.2 and prevented from sleeping; the runner cells are described in Section~\ref{sec:runners}. The system installed its updates automatically, so the cells ran on pre-release builds of macOS 27.2 (beta, beta~3 from 16 October, the release candidate from 20 October) and the last blocks, and all of RQ1b, on the final release (from 31 October); every cell is also reported by build (amendment~13). The host was shared with other work, so the CPU used by other processes was logged every minute and games were scheduled around it (amendments 8--10); from 27 October the GPU and memory were logged as well. Games were managed by fastchess v1.8.2-alpha (commit \texttt{f618e34}) at concurrency 1. Reference engines were Stockfish 19 (tag \texttt{sf\_19}, PGO build) and Lc0 0.32.1 with the same BT4 weights. \subsection{Match protocol and statistics}
\label{sec:match}
Each cell is a fixed number of game pairs: every opening is played twice with colours swapped. Openings are drawn in a seeded random order from Fishtest's \texttt{UHO\_Lichess\_4852\_v1} book of 2{,}632{,}036 unbalanced human positions~\cite{sfbooks}, with the same seed in every cell of a research question. Engines are restarted for every game and do not ponder; the \MF{} arms use a 50 ms move overhead and the reference engines their defaults (10 ms for Stockfish 19; on the runners Lc0 had 50 ms). Games are adjudicated as drawn after move 34 when both engines' scores have stayed within $\pm 20$ cp for eight consecutive moves each, and as lost after three consecutive scores beyond $\pm 600$ cp from both engines' perspectives, and are capped at 200 moves; the rules read the last score each engine reports, for the hybrid alpha-beta's except on its rare MCTS moves. Adjudications, time forfeits and other terminations are counted per cell (no game ended in a crash), and no game of a cell is excluded after the fact. Each workstation cell is split into four blocks that draw disjoint openings; the blocks of the four 30+0.3 cells are interleaved over the calendar, and later cells run block after block.

The primary estimator is the pentanomial model: each game pair scores 0, $\tfrac12$, 1, $1\tfrac12$ or 2 points, and the mean and variance of the pair score give the logistic Elo difference with a 95\% normal-approximation interval, the likelihood of superiority (LOS) and normalised Elo (nElo), which divides the score excess by its per-game standard deviation~\cite{vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}. The statistics are computed from the PGN by our own script and agree with fastchess's to rounding. The protocol fixed no smallest effect of interest: a null result is reported as no detectable effect at the achieved resolution, the half-width of the 95\% interval. Block consistency is tested with Cochran's $Q$ over the block estimates (four per workstation cell, ten per runner cell and six for RQ5a-R), with a block bootstrap that resamples, on the workstation, the measurement script's chunks, the sessions of play between scheduling pauses, and on the runners the blocks themselves.  Every workstation cell is also reported without the pairs that overlapped high outside load (amendment~8), with a pair-level regression on that load, without or with drawn forfeit pairs, and by macOS build; RQ1a also without the 92 openings the pilot and set-aside games had played (amendments 12, 13). A result that moves by more than one standard error under any of these is called load-sensitive, a convention fixed by amendment~12, not a significance test. Every runner cell is reported by runner image (amendment~15 (c)). RQ1a is the single confirmatory test; all other cells are exploratory, no family-wise correction is applied, and every planned cell is reported whether or not it ran.

\subsection{Research questions and cells}
\label{sec:rqs}
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (confirmatory for RQ1a only).] Does the shipped hybrid change strength against alpha-beta at an equal \texttt{Threads} setting? Hybrid(8) vs AB(8) at 30~s plus 0.3~s per move, 1{,}000 pairs (RQ1a); at 60+0.6, 500 pairs (RQ1b, exploratory: the regime with the most GPU participation); at 10+0.1, 1{,}000 pairs (RQ1c, exploratory: the regime in which the time-safety rule disables the transformer on most moves; played on the runners as RQ1c-R). The decision rule is the sign of RQ1a's 95\% interval; an interval spanning zero is inconclusive at this $N$, reported with the range of effects it excludes. Amendment~12, written after the interim reading of RQ1a's first two blocks and before the cell was interpreted, fixed the outcomes' meaning; it read an interval spanning zero as the GPU path not paying for itself at this budget.
  \item[RQ2 (isolation, split configuration only).] What do two threads cost, and how does the split hybrid fare given six? AB(6) vs AB(8) (RQ2a) and Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(6) (RQ2b), 500 pairs each at 30+0.3; Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(8) (RQ2c) is derived as their sum on the workstation, and on the runners the direct RQ2c-R was played and is set beside RQ2b-R plus RQ2a-R.
  \item[RQ5 (validity).] Is the alpha-beta core at parity with Stockfish 19 (AB(8) vs SF19(8) at 30+0.3, 500 pairs; RQ5c, read as a parity interval with no equivalence claim)? The protocol also asked whether the MCTS implementation is at parity with Lc0 at matched settings (MCTS(1) vs Lc0-mb1, 300 pairs; RQ5a). Played on a runner as RQ5a-R, it compares two complete search-plus-backend stacks that differ in root heuristics and backend speed (Section~\ref{sec:system}), so it is reported as an exploratory comparison, not as a validity check.
  \item[RQ6 (arbitration).] From the traces: disagreement rate, decision reasons and how often the MCTS move is chosen (RQ6a). The oracle study (RQ6b) judges 600 positions drawn from the pooled disagreements of RQ1a and RQ1b (25{,}138 and 17{,}338 records, time-safety moves excluded), stratified by game phase and by the terciles of the pooled MCTS visit counts and never by a judge's verdict (amendment~6). Two related engines judge both moves, each sharing one candidate's lineage: Stockfish 19 (60~s, both moves scored at equal depth with multi-PV, Syzygy tables) and Lc0 with the BT4 network (each candidate scored by evaluating the position after it with 30{,}000 nodes from a fresh tree). The study reports, per judge and on the positions where the judges agree, the share of positions where each candidate is better (ties within 10 cp), each move's expected-score loss (logistic, 250~cp per unit of log-odds), and the bound on what an arbiter choosing between the two root candidates could gain; a judge that both selects and scores is optimistic, so amendment~14 adds cross-judge bounds, in which one judge selects and the other scores. A bound's projected Elo is a conversion, not a prediction of game results. The positions are the workstation's; only the judging moved to the runners (Section~\ref{sec:runners}), so the rule that runner results are compared only with each other does not apply to them.
  \item[] How each outcome will be read was fixed before the oracle was read (amendment~16): if the consensus bound and both cross-judge bounds lie within a tie band of 0.01 expected-score points (the 10~cp tie threshold at an even position), \emph{little to gain}; if all lie beyond it, \emph{room under both judges}; otherwise \emph{judge-dependent or unresolved}. RQ1a's and RQ1b's positions are also reported apart, and statements about RQ1a use RQ1a's; the class is recomputed with the conversion constant halved and doubled; and any combined reading of RQ1a with the bound is exploratory. On the runners Stockfish searches with 3 threads instead of 8 and Lc0 runs on the CPU; a judge passes the stability check on the 74 positions the workstation judged with at least 0.8 equal verdicts and a correlation of at least 0.8.
  \item[RQ6d.] The MCTS-to-alpha-beta hints and the verification sub-search off together, against AB at 30+0.3, 500 pairs: that combined channel, compared with RQ1a as an across-match contrast with propagated uncertainty; played on the runners as RQ6d-R against RQ1a-R.
  \item[Microbenchmarks.] BT4 latency and throughput against batch size (M1), alpha-beta nodes/s against threads (M2), the memory footprint (M4) and, from the games, in-game MCTS evaluations per second (M3), the transformer-enabled fraction and playouts per move (M5) and the thermal state (M6).
\end{description}
On the workstation, RQ1a, RQ1b, RQ2a, RQ2b and RQ5c are complete, with M1 to M6; its oracle run was stopped at 74 of 600 positions when the measurements moved, and those positions serve only the stability check. On the runners, RQ1a-R, RQ6d-R, RQ2a-R, RQ2b-R, RQ2c-R, RQ1c-R and RQ5a-R are complete, with M1, M2, M3, M5 and M6; \pending{the oracle study RQ6b-R: status at the cut-off}. RQ3 needs more cores than a runner has, RQ4 an engine change that the freeze excludes, RQ8 (the multi-engine table) a gauntlet script that was never written (amendment~15 (g)), and RQ6c, an ablation of the predicate ladder, an arbitration switch the frozen build does not have. RQ5d is covered by RQ5c. RQ1c's always-on variant, RQ1d (the balanced-book control) and RQ5b are optional cells of the protocol's lowest tier, which it allows to be dropped; RQ7, a cell of the second tier, was not run, and no amendment records why (its suites measure tactical accuracy, not strength, and nothing here depends on them). RQ9 describes the engine's Lichess games, and the corpus the protocol names predates the freeze.

RQ2a prices the two alpha-beta threads the split gives to the GPU side on its transformer-enabled moves. AB(6) scored $-19.8$~Elo against AB(8)

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{The treatment as a bundle.} RQ1a measures the hybrid runtime as built. No cell at 30+0.3 runs the hybrid with the transformer off, so a contribution of the GPU search and a cost of the machinery around it cannot be separated; the two null controls before the freeze, at 10+0.1, were too small to settle it. \textbf{An under-provisioned GPU arm.} GPU MCTS is normally run with several workers and batched, overlapping evaluations; the arm studied here runs one worker at batch size~1, only on the moves where the transformer is enabled, so the in-game evaluations are reported next to the rates this chip reaches with batching (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). A null result would not show that a better-driven GPU search cannot help. \textbf{What generalises.} The equal-budget accounting, the oracle method and the telemetry carry over to other hardware; the absolute Elo and frequencies measured here do not, and the Neural Engine path and the CUDA build were not tested. \textbf{GPU participation.} The time-safety rule is part of the system under test: the transformer is enabled on about half of the hybrid's moves at 30+0.3 and on 1.7\% at 10+0.1 (RQ1c-R). The cells that differ in participation also differ in time control, so no dependence of the effect on participation is estimated. \textbf{Non-interference.} The design assumes that one arm does not slow the other. On the runners AB(3) reported fewer nodes per second against the hybrid arms than against AB(1); either the hybrid process slows its opponent, which would favour the hybrid in those cells, or the positions differ, and the data do not separate the two. \textbf{Tuning.} The MCTS and arbiter heuristics were tuned on tactical test positions (Bratko--Kopec and Lichess puzzles), which shapes the design but not the game results. \textbf{Adjudication.} The draw and resignation rules end a game only when both engines' scores agree that it is decided, and the 200-move cap ends it regardless; adjudication decided 1{,}811 of RQ1a's 2{,}002 games (789 draws; 498 wins for the hybrid and 524 for AB(8)). The protocol's re-run of one RQ1a block without adjudication was not run, a deviation from the protocol. \textbf{RQ5c.} Stockfish 19 kept its 10 ms move overhead against the \MF{} arm's 50 ms and is a PGO build, both slightly in its favour, as pre-registered. \textbf{Clock division.} Both arms play on the same game clock, but on transformer-enabled moves the verification runs before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move and the agreement rule can stop alpha-beta early, so the hybrid divides its clock differently; the time of every move is in the released games. Neither the CPU time of each thread nor power and energy were measured (the tools need administrator rights on this host). \textbf{Shared host.} The workstation was not idle: in the 1{,}305 minutes of play above three cores of outside load, the largest outside process was a virtual machine in 251 and a Python process (among them this study's scripts) in 445. Over the \HostGameSamples{} minutes that overlapped a game, other processes used a median of \HostGameMedianCores{} cores (95th percentile \HostGamePninetyfiveCores{}), and \HostGameOverPct\% of those minutes exceeded three cores (the gate checks the load only before a chunk starts); memory available never fell below 35\% during a cell's games. Because the arms of RQ1a and RQ2 need different numbers of free cores, load can bias as well as blur them: time forfeits came in minutes of high outside load and fell mostly on the alpha-beta arm, which favours the hybrid in RQ1a (Table~\ref{tab:health}); every cell is therefore also reported without the pairs that overlapped such minutes (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). \textbf{System updates.} The binary never changed, but four builds of macOS 27.2 were in use, so the cells are reported by build. \textbf{The runners.} The runners' neighbours on the same physical host cannot be observed, their GPU is paravirtualised and their timing jitters; fastchess forgave overruns of up to one second, and each cell is also reported without that margin. A runner has three cores, so the shipped hybrid's MCTS worker shares a core with alpha-beta, where the workstation left four efficiency cores beyond the eight threads; it also has a different GPU, less memory and a virtualised operating system, so its cells measure a different machine, not a harder version of the workstation. The runner images changed during the campaign, and each cell is reported by image. \textbf{Oracle judges.} Stockfish 19 shares the alpha-beta candidate's search and evaluation and Lc0 with BT4 the MCTS candidate's network, so each judge may favour its own lineage; Stockfish~19's networks are also trained on Leela data, so the judges are related, and only the Stockfish judge has tablebases. \textbf{Statistics.} One confirmatory test at fixed $N$; everything else is exploratory and reported in full.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On one M2 Max at 30+0.3, the shipped hybrid, which runs Lc0's BT4 transformer in an MCTS search on the GPU beside Stockfish~19's alpha-beta search, was not detectably stronger than the alpha-beta search alone at the same thread setting: the interval of $[-10.7, +2.0]$~Elo excludes gains above 2~Elo. The treatment was the hybrid runtime as a whole, so this does not separate what the GPU search adds from what the machinery around it costs, and it does not show that a better-driven GPU search could not help. At 60+0.6 the hybrid was weaker, by 40.5~Elo (95\% interval $[-49.6, -31.4]$; exploratory); that cell differs from RQ1a in time control as well as in GPU participation, and it does not identify its cause. The arbiter almost never played the MCTS move, so what the hybrid changed ran through its alpha-beta search: the root hints, the verification sub-search, the agreement rule, contention for the shared cores, memory and clock, and the machinery's own costs. The split configuration lost 17~Elo with at least as many alpha-beta threads as its opponent on every move, which shows that the hybrid as built can cost strength without the GPU search choosing a move.

On three-core virtual M1 machines (the exploratory runner cells), the shipped hybrid lost to alpha-beta at the same thread setting by 35.9~Elo at 30+0.3 and by 16.7~Elo at 10+0.1, where the arbiter never overrode alpha-beta: there the hybrid's alpha-beta search, running inside the hybrid, chose moves that did worse than plain alpha-beta's, and which part of the hybrid costs this is not separated here. The hints and verification made no detectable difference. The runners differ from the workstation in core count (no spare core for the MCTS worker), GPU, memory and virtualisation, and their cells are not compared with the workstation's.

The GPU search measured here was under-provisioned by design: one worker, batch size~1, a median of 58 evaluations per second inside games at 30+0.3 and 87 at 60+0.6 against a batch-1 ceiling of 139 and a batched throughput near 540, and an arbiter whose thresholds most moves never reach. A concurrent GPU search is worth reconsidering when the GPU side is driven harder (batched evaluations, several workers), when it does not take cores the alpha-beta search would otherwise use, and when an arbiter has evidence that the MCTS move is right often enough to override. The evidence here does not favour the first condition: RQ1b, the only cell with markedly more MCTS work per move, lost the most, although it does not test batching and differs in time control and in how often the agreement rule fired. The oracle study is the test of the third: \pending{the oracle study's result on the runners (RQ6b-R)}. The engine's CUDA build shares the search and network contracts, so the same protocol can be run on a discrete GPU, where memory is not shared and contention differs; the absolute numbers here do not transfer.

\section{Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}
\begin{description}
  \item[Time control 30+0.3.] Thirty seconds per player for the game, plus 0.3~s added after each of that player's moves.
  \item[Nodes, playouts, evaluations.] An alpha-beta node is a position visited by the search; an MCTS playout is one descent from the root to a leaf; an evaluation is one network inference, and a playout can reuse a cached evaluation.
  \item[Fail-high.] A search result at or above the upper bound of its search window; it is unproven until a re-search with a wider window confirms it.
  \item[Bench signature.] The node count of an engine's fixed benchmark search; identical counts mean identical search behaviour on those positions.
  \item[PUCT and first-play urgency.] PUCT selects the child that maximises its value plus an exploration term weighted by the network's prior; first-play urgency is the value assumed for children that have not been visited yet.
  \item[Multi-PV.] A search that reports the best $k$ moves with their scores instead of one.
  \item[Lock convoy.] A collapse of throughput in which threads queue repeatedly on one contended lock.
  \item[HalfKAv2\_hm, FullThreats.] Names of input feature sets of Stockfish's NNUE networks.
  \item[Transformer-enabled fraction.] The share of the hybrid's moves on which the time-safety rule leaves the transformer search on.
  \item[Adjudication, time forfeit.] A game ended by the match manager's draw, resignation or move-cap rule; a game lost by exceeding the clock.
  \item[Syzygy tablebases.] Exact databases of endgame positions with few pieces, used here only by the oracle judges.
  \item[Root hints and verification.] Moves passed from the MCTS root to the alpha-beta root at the start of a move; they set the order of alpha-beta's first iteration, and a short restricted alpha-beta search checks the first five of them.
  \item[Veto.] The arbiter's rule that blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks it clearly below its own move; most override predicates can lift it (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}).
\end{description}
