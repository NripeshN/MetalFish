\begin{abstract}
Can a GPU make a Stockfish-class chess engine stronger? We test one configuration of the question.
\MF{} runs a transformer Monte Carlo tree search (one worker evaluating Lc0's BT4 network at batch
size~1) on the GPU of an Apple M2 Max beside Stockfish~19's alpha-beta search on its CPU, and an arbiter
chooses between their moves. Under a protocol published before the first confirmatory game, the shipped
hybrid played 2{,}002 games against the same binary's alpha-beta search at the same thread setting (30~s
plus 0.3~s per move, unbalanced openings in colour-swapped pairs). It scored $-4.3$~Elo (95\% CI
$-10.7$ to $+2.0$): inconclusive at this sample size, with gains above 2.0~Elo excluded. The treatment
is the hybrid runtime as a whole, so the result cannot separate what the GPU search adds from what the
machinery around it costs, and a null result would not show that a better-driven GPU search cannot
help. The arbiter played the MCTS move on 7 of 25{,}138 disagreements. In exploratory cells the hybrid
lost 40.5~Elo at 60+0.6 (95\% CI $-49.6$ to $-31.4$), and a split configuration that ran alpha-beta on
six of the eight threads while the transformer was enabled lost 17.0~Elo to six-thread alpha-beta. In
exploratory cells on GitHub-hosted virtual M1 machines with three cores, analysed only against each other, the hybrid lost
35.9~Elo at 30+0.3, where switching off
the MCTS search's hints and verification sub-search changed the result by $+9.8 \pm 14.8$~Elo (no effect detected, with an interval too wide to rule that channel out as a main contributor to the deficit), and 16.7~Elo at 10+0.1, where every move it played was alpha-beta's. On the workstation, against Stockfish~19, the alpha-beta core scored $+1.4$~Elo ($-7.3$ to $+10.1$),
no difference detected. On 600 disagreement positions from the workstation's RQ1a and RQ1b games, judged on the runners by Stockfish~19 and by Lc0 with the BT4 network (RQ6b-R), an arbiter choosing between the two root candidates at decision time could have gained little: where both judges agree, the bound is 0.0009 expected-score points per disagreement position (95\% interval 0.0005 to 0.0014), and it and both cross-judge bounds lie within the registered tie band of 0.01, with Stockfish~19's verdicts those of its runner configuration (it failed the stability check). The ratings
reported in an earlier conference chapter on the system do not hold; a Correction has been prepared for
its publisher and not yet filed. All games, traces, logs and scripts \iffinal\ifanon will be\else are\fi\else will be\fi{} released in a public deposit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The strongest chess programs follow one of two designs. One is alpha-beta search with an efficiently updatable neural-network evaluation (NNUE), exemplified by Stockfish~\cite{stockfish,nasu2018,sf12}, which searches tens of millions of nodes per second on a CPU. The other is Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) guided by a deep policy--value network, the approach of AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018} and of Leela Chess Zero (Lc0)~\cite{lc0,monroe2026}, which evaluates far fewer positions (AlphaZero about 60{,}000 per second against Stockfish's 60 million~\cite{silver2018}) and relies on the quality of each. Both searches can run on the same position concurrently, with an arbiter between their answers, on any machine with a CPU and a GPU; on systems-on-chip such as Apple's M-series~\cite{applem2}, where the CPU and the GPU share one memory~\cite{appleunified}, this is compact, and the two searches compete for shared resources, a candidate cost with which the in-game rates are consistent (Section~\ref{sec:system}). Whether that adds playing strength to a Stockfish-class search sits beside a general question, how to split test-time compute between a fast search with a cheap evaluation (here more than ten million positions per second on eight cores) and a slower learned evaluator (here at most 139 positions per second at batch size~1); test-time compute has been traded against training compute in Hex~\cite{jones2021}, but we know of no controlled measurement of that split in chess. The shipped hybrid adds an accelerator to an alpha-beta search that keeps all its threads; it does not divide one budget.

\ifanon An earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\else Our earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\fi{} against the engine's source and the retained records showed that its central quantitative claims do not hold: each rating was an average, over opponents, of an assumed opponent rating plus the Elo difference implied by a five-game score; the alpha-beta component is Stockfish's own search; and several implementation statements were wrong (Section~\ref{sec:chapter}\ifanon\else; a Correction has been prepared for the publisher and not yet filed\fi). This paper is the controlled study the chapter lacked. It updates the engine (Section~\ref{sec:prep}), freezes it, and asks one pre-registered question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does running an Lc0-class transformer MCTS (one worker, batch size~1) on the GPU of an Apple M2 Max concurrently with a Stockfish-class alpha-beta search on its CPU, with a final arbiter that chooses between their root moves, change playing strength relative to the alpha-beta search alone, at equal wall-clock and equal CPU-thread budget?}
\end{quote}

Equal budget means the same \texttt{Threads} setting for both arms; the hybrid's MCTS worker is an extra thread that waits on the GPU during each evaluation. ``Lc0-class'' refers to the network, not to Lc0's search configuration, from which \MF{}'s MCTS also departs (Section~\ref{sec:system}). The treatment is the shipped hybrid runtime as a whole, which can change the outcome through five channels: the MCTS root hints and the verification sub-search; the agreement rule, which can end a move early; contention between the two searches for cores, memory and the clock; the costs of the machinery that arise even when no GPU search runs; and the arbiter's override, which never selected the MCTS move in the frozen build's gate L14 (0 of 1{,}344 disagreements) or in the pilot (0 of 1{,}165). Two exploratory cells (RQ2b on the M2 Max, RQ1c-R on the runners) show that the hybrid can cost strength with no MCTS move played, without separating these channels, and RQ1a cannot separate them either; whether arbitration could be worth anything is the question of an oracle study (RQ6b-R) that judges the disagreements with two engines. %src: results/paper_v2/sprt/L14_hybrid_lean_vs_ab8_30p03/moves.jsonl; results/paper_v2/RQ1a_pilot/moves.jsonl
The protocol was merged into the public repository before the first confirmatory game, every amendment carries its merge time (Section~\ref{sec:amend}), the engine was frozen at a tagged commit, every number is traced to a retained artifact, and every table and figure is generated from the artifacts by a script.

\paragraph{Results.} Over 2{,}002 games at 30+0.3, the shipped hybrid scored $-4.3$~Elo against AB(8), with a 95\% interval of $[-10.7, +2.0]$: inconclusive at this $N$ by the registered rule, with no detectable effect at the achieved resolution of $\pm 6.3$~Elo. Amendment~12, which fixed what this outcome means, merged about four hours after an interim reading of RQ1a's first two blocks ($-9.4 \pm 9.1$) had been committed and before the cell was interpreted; its reading, that the GPU path does not pay for itself at this budget, is a clause added after that interim, no margin of practical relevance or cost was measured, and the data support only that gains above 2.0~Elo are excluded. The arbiter played the MCTS move on 7 of the 25{,}138 moves on which the two searches disagreed. In the earlier split configuration (RQ2a and RQ2b, exploratory), two of the eight alpha-beta threads were worth 19.8~Elo to plain alpha-beta (24.7 at zero outside load), and the split hybrid lost a further 17.0~Elo to alpha-beta on six threads with at least as many alpha-beta threads as its opponent on every move. At 60+0.6 (RQ1b, exploratory) the hybrid scored $-40.5$~Elo, $[-49.6, -31.4]$, in a cell that differs from RQ1a in time control and in GPU participation.

The remaining measurements moved to GitHub-hosted virtual M1 machines with three cores (amendment~15; Section~\ref{sec:runners}), whose exploratory cells are compared only with each other. There the shipped hybrid lost to alpha-beta at the same thread setting by 35.9~Elo at 30+0.3 ($[-46.2, -25.6]$; RQ1a-R) and by 16.7~Elo at 10+0.1 ($[-24.4, -9.0]$; RQ1c-R); at 10+0.1 every move it played was alpha-beta's, so on such a machine the hybrid as built can cost strength without the GPU search choosing a move. The oracle study on the runners (RQ6b-R) finds little to gain: the consensus bound is 0.0009 [0.0005, 0.0014] expected-score points per disagreement position, and it and both cross-judge bounds lie within the tie band of 0.01, with Stockfish~19's verdicts those of its runner configuration (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled methodology, fixed in advance in a time-stamped public protocol, for evaluating hybrid or ensemble engines that share one machine: a fair-resource policy, telemetry of GPU participation, host-load logging with a scheduling gate, isolation cells and an oracle analysis with reading rules fixed before it was read (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A corrected as-built description of \MF{}, whose ratings in the earlier chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} do not hold (a Correction has been prepared for its publisher and not yet filed; Section~\ref{sec:chapter}), including where its MCTS departs from Lc0's, with the measured batch-1 ceiling of its GPU evaluator and the in-game evaluation and node rates, and how they differ between configurations that leave different numbers of cores free beside alpha-beta (Sections~\ref{sec:system} and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Measurements of the shipped hybrid runtime against alpha-beta at an equal \texttt{Threads} setting, at two time controls on the M2 Max and two on three-core runners; of the hints-and-verification channel, on the runners; of the split configuration as built against alpha-beta on the threads it keeps; and of the alpha-beta core against Stockfish~19 as a validity condition (a parity interval, with no equivalence claim) (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Two exploratory cells, the split configuration on the M2 Max (RQ2b) and the shipped hybrid at 10+0.1 on the runners (RQ1c-R), show that the machinery can cost strength with no MCTS move played; the workstation MCTS arm's standing against Lc0 is not established.
  \item The design of an oracle analysis that bounds, under two stated judges, what any arbiter choosing between the two root candidates at decision time could gain, with the arbitration telemetry of every hybrid move (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}), and its result on the runners: little to gain under either judge, with Stockfish~19's verdicts those of its runner configuration (RQ6b-R).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The two paradigms}
Stockfish combines alpha-beta pruning~\cite{knuth1975} with aspiration windows, null-move and futility pruning, late-move reductions and a shared transposition table, and since 2020 it has evaluated positions with an NNUE network~\cite{nasu2018,sf12}, whose first layer is updated incrementally as moves are made and unmade. MCTS~\cite{coulom2006,kocsis2006,browne2012} with PUCT selection~\cite{rosin2011} and a policy--value network, as in AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018}, grows a tree whose leaves the network evaluates, so its quality depends on how many evaluations fit into the time for a move. Lc0 is an open-source engine in this tradition whose current networks are transformers~\cite{monroe2026,lc0}, among them the BT4 network used here; transformers have also been trained to play without explicit search~\cite{ruoss2024}. On a GPU the evaluation rate is governed by batching: at small batch sizes fixed framework overhead dominates~\cite{fernandez2023}, and batched MCTS keeps its statistics usable with a virtual loss~\cite{chaslot2008} or by correcting selection for the simulations in flight~\cite{liu2020wuuct}. Game-tree search has also been run on GPUs, for MCTS~\cite{rocki2011,barriga2014} and for alpha-beta~\cite{strnad2011}; here the GPU only evaluates a network for a search driven from the CPU. Co-executing one workload on a unified-memory Apple chip's CPU and GPU helps in some phases of language-model inference and not in others~\cite{mohite2026}.  The paradigms are not independent in what they know: Stockfish's networks have been trained partly on positions evaluated by Leela Chess Zero since Stockfish~14~\cite{sf14}, and all of Stockfish~19's training positions were rescored with a strong Leela network~\cite{stockfish}. A transformer consulted a few dozen times per move may therefore add less independent information to Stockfish~19 than the diversity results below suggest; this redundancy and the under-provisioning of the GPU side (Section~\ref{sec:system}) predict different outcomes for a GPU side driven harder, and this study does not distinguish them.

\subsection{Combining minimax and MCTS}
Algorithmic hybrids embed one search inside the other: shallow minimax searches in the phases of MCTS~\cite{baier2013,baier2015,baier2018}, an implicit minimax value backed up beside the Monte Carlo average~\cite{lanctot2014}, rollout formulations of alpha-beta~\cite{huang2015,baier2017}, and minimax depth varied dynamically~\cite{ji2023}. A common motivation is the weakness of sampling-based search in tactical positions, where UCT can miss shallow traps that minimax finds~\cite{ramanujan2010}, although in synthetic games UCT can show lookahead pathology, to which minimax is similarly susceptible~\cite{nguyen2024}. In the AlphaZero setting, an alpha-beta phase has been added to an AlphaZero-style player~\cite{chen2019}, a mixture of experts combined with MCTS in chess~\cite{helfenstein2024}, and minimax-based learning shown to remain competitive~\cite{cohen2023}. Engine practice has its own precedents: Scorpio's MCTS with alpha-beta rollouts or priors~\cite{scorpio}; Komodo's MCTS mode, expanded from tiny alpha-beta searches~\cite{komodo}; a 2017 Stockfish branch with a Monte Carlo search~\cite{sfmcts2017}; the lc0-stockfish-hybrid project, which uses Stockfish as a filter inside Lc0's selection~\cite{lc0sfhybrid}; the MCTS engines Ceres and Allie on Lc0 networks~\cite{ceres,allie} and Stoofvlees~II, the 2023 World Computer Chess Champion~\cite{pascutto2025}; and ShashChess's experimental MCTS option~\cite{shashchess}. 

\subsection{Choosing among several programs' moves}
Selecting a move from several programs' proposals predates neural networks. Alth\"ofer's multiple-choice systems give a human decider the candidate moves of two or more programs~\cite{althofer2003,althofer2004}; Clever~\&~Smart, an automatic version in which two versions of Shredder passed their proposals and search data to a control program that chose the move and managed the clock, finished second at the 1998 International Paderborn Computer Chess Championship~\cite{cleversmart}. In computer shogi, consultation by majority vote~\cite{obata2011} and an optimistic rule that plays the move of the program reporting the best evaluation~\cite{sugiyama2011} were compared in controlled matches, and consultation was used in the distributed system Akara 2010~\cite{hoki2013}. In computer Go, a team of diverse, individually weaker programs can outplay a uniform team of stronger ones~\cite{marcolino2013,marcolino2014}, which suggests that the positions where searches of different paradigms disagree are where an arbiter could gain. The hybrid studied here is a two-member consultation run concurrently on one chip under an equal \texttt{Threads} setting; with two members a vote cannot break a disagreement, and the arbiter defaults to the alpha-beta move unless gated predicates favour the MCTS move, which is closer to a veto rule than to the optimistic rule. What is new is a controlled, equal-budget measurement of such a consultation run as a whole, including what the consulting search does through the alpha-beta search, and an engine-judged bound on what per-move arbitration between the two paradigms could gain.

\subsection{Measuring strength}
Elo differences between near-equal engines are small relative to game-to-game noise, and the conventions matter as much as the games. Fishtest's methodology~\cite{vdbergh_gsprt,vdbergh_rw,vdbergh_nelo,fishtest} plays colour-swapped game pairs from unbalanced human openings (UHO)~\cite{sfbooks,pohl2024} so draws do not swamp the signal, and reports pentanomial statistics and normalised Elo; the book choice is debated~\cite{uhocritique}. Draw modelling~\cite{szczecinski2020} and uncertainty in paired comparisons~\cite{glickman1999} are the statistical background; ratings from public lists are specific to their reference hardware and time control~\cite{ccrl} and cannot be imported to another machine; in its seventeenth season the Top Chess Engine Championship gave CPU and GPU engines separate lower leagues on their own hardware~\cite{haworth2020}. Engines and tablebases have long served as judges of move quality~\cite{guid2006,regan2011,anderson2017}, and their verdicts depend on how deep they search~\cite{ferreira2013}.

\section{The system as built}
\label{sec:system}

\MF{} is a single UCI binary with three modes selected by options: plain alpha-beta (\texttt{AB}), stand-alone transformer MCTS, and the hybrid that runs both concurrently. This section describes the engine as frozen for this study (\ifanon source and binary in the archive~\depositcite{}\else repository \url{https://github.com/NripeshN/MetalFish}, tag \texttt{paper-v2-freeze-rc2} at commit \texttt{8c0661d}, built from the source of commit \texttt{365f36a}\fi; the binary was rebuilt once against an upgraded system library, Section~\ref{sec:amend}); Section~\ref{sec:chapter} lists where the earlier chapter's description differed. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the threads and data flows.

\begin{figure}[t]

Threads and their macOS quality-of-service classes: the $T$ alpha-beta threads are user-interactive; the MCTS worker, an alpha-beta and an MCTS control thread and the coordinator, created for each move with the transformer enabled, are user-initiated, so the scheduler decides the worker's core and its share of the CPU (on the workstation the eight alpha-beta threads leave the four efficiency cores free). \subsection{Alpha-beta component}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish's search and NNUE evaluation: a port of Stockfish 19 with its SFNNv16 network (\texttt{nn-1a298aa575a0.nnue}), whose \texttt{bench} command reproduces the upstream signature of 2{,}497{,}913 nodes, so the search is node-for-node Stockfish's. %src: results/paper_v2/queue_paper_log.txt (bench lines 25, 60, 98, 105); upstream tag sf_19; 03 §0
Its local changes are few: the root accepts move-ordering hints from the MCTS component, the previous principal variation is kept when a search stops on an unproven fail-high, the hybrid can set the size of the thread pool, and there are thread quality-of-service classes and small hints and checks. NNUE runs on the CPU by design. On the test machine the frozen binary searches 1.47 million nodes/s on one thread, 12.8 million on eight and 15.1 million on all twelve cores at fixed depth (Figure~\ref{fig:m2}). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/ab_scaling_frozen.json (depth 16, median of 5; 08 §B M2 frozen build)

\subsection{MCTS component and neural-network backend}
The MCTS component is a modified Lc0-style search. It reads Lc0's network format and uses PUCT selection with Lc0's constants and policy softmax temperature below the root (Appendix~\ref{app:mcts}), tree reuse between moves and a cache of network outputs keyed on the position. It departs from Lc0 0.32.1's defaults at the root in both modes: a root policy temperature of 1.6 instead of Lc0's single 1.359, and a root tactical capture probe that can redirect selection to particular captures when the root has 16 to 600 visits. The stand-alone mode, MCTS(1) of RQ5a-R, departs further: a root cpuct of 2.4, a smart-pruning factor of 0.5 instead of 1.33, a KLD-gain stopping rule that Lc0 has off by default, Lc0's smooth time manager where Lc0 defaults to its legacy one, and three root selection heuristics; the hybrid switches those heuristics and the KLD rule off (Appendix~\ref{app:mcts}). Tuned on tactical test positions, these heuristics shape the MCTS candidate, and so the one the oracle judges, at the median 38 to 101 playouts per transformer-enabled move of RQ1a and RQ1b. The BT4 transformer (\texttt{BT4-1024x15x32h}, 15 encoder blocks, 32 heads, 1024-wide; 383 MB of weights) is built once as a Metal Performance Shaders graph~\cite{mpsgraph} with an FP16 body and FP32 inputs and outputs, and one evaluation is in flight at a time. %src: 365f36a src/mcts/search_params.h 25-107, src/uci/engine.cpp 191-222, src/uci/uci.cpp 1040-1090, 1139-1153, 1247-1270; Lc0 v0.32.1 src/neural/shared_params.cc:69, src/search/classic/params.cc 543-565, src/search/classic/stoppers/common.cc 89-92

The batch-1 latency of this implementation is the ceiling of the design as built. With the frozen binary, a single BT4 evaluation takes 7.21~ms in FP16 (139 positions/s), the precision the engine uses, and 8.56~ms in FP32; a batch of 8 takes 18.9~ms (422 positions/s) and a batch of 64 takes 119~ms (538 positions/s), so batching could raise throughput almost fourfold (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). The engine as shipped does not batch; whether batching, its statistics kept usable as above~\cite{chaslot2008,liu2020wuuct}, would help it inside a game is not tested here. %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency_frozen.json (08 §B, M1 frozen build); src/uci/engine.cpp:140 (NNMetalFP16 true)
Figure~\ref{fig:m1} also shows the search rate of Lc0 0.32.1 with its Metal backend and the same weights (217 nodes/s at minibatch~1, 763 at 32); its raw-evaluation benchmark failed on this machine, and search nodes are not network evaluations, so the rates are not compared. %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency_frozen.json (lc0 nps_steady_median; 08 §B M1 frozen build)
Inside a game the MCTS worker shares the chip with eight alpha-beta threads. The halving of its in-game rate (Section~\ref{sec:results}) is consistent with competition for cores, the chip's system-level cache, memory bandwidth or its shared power and thermal budget; none of these was measured, and two exploratory cells (RQ2b on the M2 Max, RQ1c-R on the runners) lost strength with no MCTS move played (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Hybrid runtime and arbiter}
\label{sec:arbiter}
On every move the coordinator starts the alpha-beta search and the MCTS search on the same position. Alpha-beta keeps all $T$ threads and the MCTS worker runs as an extra thread (\texttt{HybridOversubscribe}). Stockfish's clock-based time manager sets alpha-beta's time (\texttt{HybridFollowABTime}), and the coordinator ends the move when the alpha-beta search returns, with two qualifications described below: the verification sub-search, which adds time before the time manager starts, and the agreement rule, which can stop alpha-beta early. The MCTS search is otherwise limited by the coordinator's own per-move budget (the remaining clock divided by 30 plus three quarters of the increment, within fixed bounds) less the move overhead. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 468-500, 2849-2855, 4037-4042, 4124-4141; hybrid_search.h 36-141; L14 engines.log.gz ("subsearch stop grace expired": 0)

At the start of a move the alpha-beta side reads up to eight moves from the MCTS root (the subtree kept from the previous move, or nothing), which reorder its root moves for the first iteration only. When the coordinator's budget is at least one second, the first five hints are verified by a restricted alpha-beta search of $\min(240~\text{ms}, \text{budget}/8)$. The verification shares the transposition and history tables with the main search, feeds two of the arbiter's predicates, is spent from the game clock before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move, and replaces state that the time manager carries from one search to the next. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 572-597, 3102-3176, 3537-3669, 3829-3864; search/search.cpp 59-98, 275-277

When both searches name the same move, it is played. When they disagree, the arbiter plays alpha-beta's move unless one of 36 predicates, tested in a fixed order, selects the MCTS move; 31 can fire under the tested settings (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}). A veto blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks its own move first and has searched the MCTS move and found it worse by a margin, or barely searched it; 24 of the 31 predicates can override the veto. The veto assumes that such a move is more often a tactical oversight of the shallow MCTS search than a judgement alpha-beta misses. Most predicates need at least 50 visits in the current move, many 100 to 300 and two 700 or 5{,}000, while the shipped worker reached a median of 38 playouts per transformer-enabled move in RQ1a; most of the ladder is therefore out of reach at 30+0.3 by construction, and the rarity of MCTS moves reflects the thresholds, not only the MCTS candidates' quality, which is what the oracle study is designed to judge (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}). The MCTS score that the predicates compare with alpha-beta's is $300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$, a conversion of the MCTS value calibrated to neither Lc0's nor Stockfish's centipawn scale.

A time-safety rule disables the transformer for a move when the estimated budget is below 400~ms or the remaining clock below 3~s; the move is then plain alpha-beta on the full thread pool, and the engine logs the rule but writes no decision trace. An agreement rule ends a move early when both searches have named the same move in three consecutive polls (the coordinator reads both searches' published states every 0.5~ms while both run; the MCTS side publishes its state every 50~ms) after a quarter of the coordinator's budget, with alpha-beta at depth 10 and at least 100 MCTS playouts (150 in positions the position classifier rates as highly complex); the classifier can only lengthen the coordinator's budget, but on the moves this rule stops it changes when alpha-beta is stopped. The rule ended 660 of the 68{,}456 transformer-enabled moves of RQ1a early (1.0\%) and 6{,}485 of the 38{,}645 of the split hybrid in RQ2b (16.8\%); its time saving was not measured directly. %src: results/paper_v2/{RQ1a,RQ2b}/engines.log ("engines agree, stopping early"; tables/flags.md); 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 1860-1873, 4058-4082, 4946-4951, 5288-5306, 5366-5480; classifier.cpp

The network is loaded and warmed up before the first \texttt{readyok}, so the game clock does not pay for initialisation. \section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Principles and fair-resource policy}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish, so its strength is not a contribution and is never compared across machines. Every primary comparison is \emph{same binary, same machine, same wall-clock, same \texttt{Threads} setting}, with the hybrid runtime against plain alpha-beta. The \texttt{Threads} setting is a nominal budget: it equalises alpha-beta's threads and the clock, not the CPU time of the hybrid's extra MCTS worker (amendment~3), which was not measured, nor the GPU and memory the hybrid also uses, nor how it divides its clock (Section~\ref{sec:arbiter}). The protocol~\depositcite{} fixed the hypotheses, cells, sample sizes, statistics and decision rules before data collection: its frozen version (SHA-256 of the protocol file: \texttt{03020292ae3441df} \texttt{5a0eb2eab1e67403} \texttt{a0722a7ee85fcb59} \texttt{0dfac04e852a89f2}) was merged into the public repository on 14 October 2026 at 06:46~UTC\ifanon\else{} (pull request~\#77, a server-side time stamp)\fi, and the first confirmatory game started at 08:09~UTC. The registration is the author's own, in a public repository, not in a third-party registry (amendments: Section~\ref{sec:amend}).

\subsection{Match protocol and statistics}
\label{sec:match}
Each cell is a fixed number of game pairs: every opening is played twice with colours swapped. Openings are drawn in a seeded random order from Fishtest's \texttt{UHO\_Lichess\_4852\_v1} book of 2{,}632{,}036 unbalanced human positions~\cite{sfbooks}, with the same seed in every cell of a research question. Engines are restarted for every game and do not ponder; the \MF{} arms use a 50 ms move overhead and the reference engines their defaults (10 ms for Stockfish 19; on the runners Lc0 had 50 ms). Games are adjudicated as drawn after move 34 when both engines' scores have stayed within $\pm 20$ cp for eight consecutive moves each, and as lost after three consecutive scores beyond $\pm 600$ cp from both engines' perspectives, and are capped at 200 moves; the rules read the last score each engine reports, for the hybrid alpha-beta's except on its rare MCTS moves. Adjudications, time forfeits and other terminations are counted per cell (no game ended in a crash), and no game of a cell is excluded after the fact. Each workstation cell is split into four blocks that draw disjoint openings; the blocks of the four 30+0.3 cells are interleaved over the calendar, and later cells run block after block.

The primary estimator is the pentanomial model: each game pair scores 0, $\tfrac12$, 1, $1\tfrac12$ or 2 points, and the mean and variance of the pair score give the logistic Elo difference with a 95\% normal-approximation interval ($\pm$ denotes its half-width throughout), the likelihood of superiority (LOS) and normalised Elo (nElo), which divides the score excess by its per-game standard deviation~\cite{vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}. The protocol fixed no smallest effect of interest: a null result is reported as no detectable effect at the achieved resolution, the half-width of the 95\% interval. Block consistency is tested with Cochran's $Q$ over the block estimates (four per workstation cell, ten per runner cell and six for RQ5a-R), with a block bootstrap that resamples, on the workstation, the measurement script's chunks, the sessions of play between scheduling pauses, and on the runners the blocks themselves. A percentile bootstrap over six or ten blocks has no small-sample correction, so each runner cell and contrast also has a Student $t$ interval over its blocks (amendment~17, post hoc). Every workstation cell is also reported without the pairs that overlapped high outside load (amendment~8), with a pair-level regression on that load, without the pairs that contain a forfeit or with forfeits scored as draws, and by macOS build; RQ1a also without the 92 openings the pilot and set-aside games had played (amendments 12, 13). A result that moves by more than one standard error under any of these is called load-sensitive, a convention fixed by amendment~12, not a significance test. Every runner cell is reported by runner image (amendment~15 (c)). RQ1a is the single confirmatory test; all other cells are exploratory, no family-wise correction is applied, and every planned cell is reported, run or not.

\subsection{Research questions and cells}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (confirmatory for RQ1a only).] Does the shipped hybrid change strength against alpha-beta at an equal \texttt{Threads} setting?   \item[RQ2 (isolation, split configuration only).] What do two threads cost, and how does the split hybrid fare given six? AB(6) vs AB(8) (RQ2a) and Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(6) (RQ2b), 500 pairs each at 30+0.3; Hybrid-split(8) vs AB(8) (RQ2c) is derived as their sum on the workstation, and on the runners the direct RQ2c-R was played and is set beside RQ2b-R plus RQ2a-R.

Played on a runner as RQ5a-R, it compares two complete search-plus-backend stacks that differ in root heuristics and backend speed (Section~\ref{sec:system}) at minibatch~1, so it is reported as an exploratory comparison, not as a validity check; it proved time-sensitive, and the runner swapped (Section~\ref{sec:runner-results}).
  \item[RQ6 (arbitration).] From the traces: disagreement rate, decision reasons and how often the MCTS move is chosen (RQ6a). The oracle study (RQ6b) judges 600 positions drawn from the pooled disagreements of RQ1a and RQ1b (25{,}138 and 17{,}338 records, time-safety moves excluded), stratified by game phase and by the terciles of the pooled MCTS visit counts, never by a judge's verdict (amendment~6). Two related engines judge both moves, each sharing one candidate's lineage: Stockfish 19 (60~s, both moves scored at equal depth with multi-PV, Syzygy tables) and Lc0 with the BT4 network (each candidate scored by evaluating the position after it with 30{,}000 nodes from a fresh tree). On the runners Stockfish searches with 3 threads instead of 8 and Lc0 runs on the CPU; a judge passes the stability check on the 74 positions the workstation judged with at least 0.8 equal verdicts and a correlation of at least 0.8, and for Stockfish the check is also one of sensitivity to search depth, 3 threads against 8 (amendment~16 (e)).

RQ1a measures the hybrid runtime as built, a bundle of the GPU search and the machinery around it; with no sham arm at 30+0.3, it cannot separate a contribution of the one from a cost of the other.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\subsection{What is measured}
\textbf{The treatment as a bundle.} RQ1a measures the hybrid runtime as built: the coordinator, the verification sub-search, the agreement rule, the division of the clock, the resident network and the GPU search together. No cell at 30+0.3 runs the hybrid with the transformer off, so a contribution of the GPU search and a cost of the machinery around it cannot be separated; the two null controls before the freeze (100 games at 30+0.3 on the build before the cache cap, and 301 games at 10+0.1 without preloading the network) were too small to settle it.

\textbf{An under-provisioned GPU arm.} GPU MCTS normally runs several workers with batched, overlapping evaluations; this arm runs one worker at batch size~1, so a null result would not show that a better-driven GPU search cannot help.

\textbf{GPU participation.} The time-safety rule is part of the system under test: the transformer is enabled on about half of the hybrid's moves at 30+0.3 and on 1.7\% at 10+0.1 (RQ1c-R). The cells that differ in participation also differ in time control, so no dependence of the effect on participation is estimated.

\textbf{Tuning.} The heuristics of the MCTS search, a modified Lc0-style search (Appendix~\ref{app:mcts}), and of the arbiter were tuned on tactical test positions (Bratko--Kopec and Lichess puzzles), which shapes the design but does not bias the game measurements.

\textbf{What generalises.} The equal-budget accounting, the oracle method and the telemetry carry over to other hardware; the absolute Elo and frequencies measured here do not, and the Neural Engine path and the CUDA build were not tested.

\subsection{Match conditions}
\textbf{Non-interference.} The design assumes that one arm does not slow the other. On the runners AB(3) reported fewer nodes per second against the hybrid arms than against AB(1); either the hybrid process slows its opponent, which would favour the hybrid in those cells, or the positions differ, and the data do not separate the two.

\textbf{Adjudication.} The draw and resignation rules end a game only when both engines' scores agree that it is decided, and the 200-move cap ends it regardless; adjudication decided 1{,}811 of RQ1a's 2{,}002 games (789 draws; 498 wins for the hybrid and 524 for AB(8)). The protocol's re-run of one RQ1a block without adjudication was not run, a deviation from the protocol.

\textbf{Opening book.} UHO openings make differences measurable but exaggerate Elo spreads relative to balanced books (hence nElo), and the book critique applies~\cite{uhocritique}. Play from them turns on converting or defending an edge, where calculation may count for more than the judgement a neural MCTS offers; the balanced-book control (RQ1d) was not run, so every result is for this book.

\textbf{Clock division.} Both arms play on the same game clock, but on transformer-enabled moves the verification runs before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move and the agreement rule can stop alpha-beta early, so the hybrid divides its clock differently; the time of every move is in the released games. Neither the CPU time of each thread nor power and energy were measured (the tools need administrator rights on this host).

\textbf{Shared host.} The workstation was not idle: in the 1{,}305 minutes of play above three cores of outside load, the largest outside process was a virtual machine in 251 and a Python process (among them this study's scripts) in 445. Over the \HostGameSamples{} minutes that overlapped a game, other processes used a median of \HostGameMedianCores{} cores (95th percentile \HostGamePninetyfiveCores{}), and \HostGameOverPct\% of those minutes exceeded three cores (the gate checks the load only before a chunk starts); memory available never fell below 35\% during a cell's games. Because the arms of RQ1a and RQ2 need different numbers of free cores, load can bias as well as blur them: time forfeits came in minutes of high outside load and fell mostly on the alpha-beta arm, which favours the hybrid in RQ1a (Table~\ref{tab:health}); every cell is therefore also reported without the pairs that overlapped such minutes (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).

\textbf{System updates.} The re-frozen binary never changed after amendment~7, but four builds of macOS 27.2 were in use, so the cells are reported by build.

\subsection{The runners}
The runners' neighbours on the same physical host cannot be observed, their GPU is paravirtualised and their timing jitters; fastchess forgave overruns of up to one second, and each cell is also reported without that margin. A runner has three cores, so the shipped hybrid's MCTS worker shares a core with alpha-beta, where the workstation left four efficiency cores beyond the eight threads; it also has a different GPU, less memory and a virtualised operating system, so its cells measure a different machine, not a harder version of the workstation. The runner images changed during the campaign, and each cell is reported by image. The workstation MCTS arm's standing against Lc0 is not established: the only comparison, RQ5a-R, is an exploratory one of two complete stacks at minibatch~1 on a runner, where \MF{}'s backend was the faster one; the stacks differ in root heuristics (Appendix~\ref{app:mcts}), the result was time-sensitive and the runner swapped.

\subsection{Oracle and statistics}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On one M2 Max at 30+0.3, the shipped hybrid, running Lc0's BT4 transformer in a modified Lc0-style MCTS search on the GPU beside Stockfish~19's alpha-beta search, was not detectably stronger than the alpha-beta search alone at the same thread setting; gains above 2~Elo are excluded. The treatment was the hybrid runtime as a whole, with no sham arm at 30+0.3, so this neither separates what the GPU search adds from what the machinery around it costs nor shows that a better-driven GPU search could not help. At 60+0.6 the hybrid was 40.5~Elo weaker (exploratory), in a cell that differs from RQ1a in time control and in GPU participation. The arbiter almost never played the MCTS move, so what the hybrid changed ran through its alpha-beta search: the root hints, the verification sub-search, the agreement rule, competition for the shared cores, memory and clock, and the machinery's own costs. The split configuration (RQ2b, exploratory) lost 17~Elo on the M2 Max with at least as many alpha-beta threads as its opponent on every move: the hybrid as built can cost strength without the GPU search choosing a move.

On three-core virtual M1 machines (the exploratory runner cells), the shipped hybrid lost to alpha-beta at the same thread setting by 35.9~Elo at 30+0.3 and by 16.7~Elo at 10+0.1. At 10+0.1 the arbiter never overrode alpha-beta, so the hybrid's own alpha-beta search chose moves that did worse than plain alpha-beta's, for reasons not separated here; at 30+0.3, switching off the hints and verification made no detectable difference ($+9.8 \pm 14.8$~Elo; $+9.9 \pm 19.7$ paired by block), an interval too wide to rule that channel out as a main contributor to the deficit.

The shipped hybrid adds an accelerator to an alpha-beta search that keeps its threads, so it is one configuration beside the question of how to split test-time compute, not a point on that trade-off; and its GPU search was under-provisioned by design: one worker, batch size~1, a median of 58 evaluations per second inside games at 30+0.3 and 87 at 60+0.6 against a batch-1 ceiling of 139 and a batched throughput near 540, and an arbiter whose thresholds most moves never reach. A concurrent GPU search is worth reconsidering when the GPU side is driven harder (batched evaluations, several workers, a faster batch-1 path), when it does not take cores the alpha-beta search would otherwise use (an explicit policy for the worker's share of the CPU), and when an arbiter has evidence that the MCTS move is right often enough to override. The evidence here does not favour the first condition: RQ1b, the only shipped-hybrid cell with markedly more MCTS work per move (92 against 36 evaluations), scored $-40.5$~Elo (exploratory; no contrast with RQ1a was registered), although it does not test batching and differs in time control and in how often the agreement rule fired. The oracle study is the test of the third: judged by Stockfish~19 and by Lc0 with the BT4 network, an arbiter choosing between the two root candidates could have gained little on the positions sampled, with Stockfish~19's verdicts those of its runner configuration (it failed the stability check). Where both judges agree the bound is 0.0009 [0.0005, 0.0014] expected-score points per disagreement position. The third condition is therefore not met by this pair of searches at the budgets tested. The engine's CUDA build shares the search and network contracts, so the protocol can be repeated on a discrete GPU.

\section{Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}
\begin{description}
  \item[Time control 30+0.3.] Thirty seconds per player for the game, plus 0.3~s added after each of that player's moves.
  \item[Nodes, playouts, evaluations.] An alpha-beta node is a position visited by the search; an MCTS playout is one descent from the root to a leaf; an evaluation is one network inference, and a playout can reuse a cached evaluation.
  \item[Fail-high.] A search result at or above the upper bound of its search window; it is unproven until a re-search with a wider window confirms it.
  \item[Bench signature.] The node count of an engine's fixed benchmark search; identical counts mean identical search behaviour on those positions.
  \item[PUCT and first-play urgency.] PUCT selects the child that maximises its value plus an exploration term weighted by the network's prior; first-play urgency is the value assumed for children that have not been visited yet.
  \item[Multi-PV.] A search that reports the best $k$ moves with their scores instead of one.
  \item[Lock convoy.] A collapse of throughput in which threads queue repeatedly on one contended lock.
  \item[HalfKAv2\_hm, FullThreats.] Names of input feature sets of Stockfish's NNUE networks.
  \item[Transformer-enabled fraction.] The share of the hybrid's moves on which the time-safety rule leaves the transformer search on.
  \item[Adjudication, time forfeit.] A game ended by the match manager's draw, resignation or move-cap rule; a game lost by exceeding the clock.
  \item[Syzygy tablebases.] Exact databases of endgame positions with few pieces, used here only by the oracle's Stockfish judge.
  \item[Root hints and verification.] Moves passed from the MCTS root to the alpha-beta root at the start of a move; they set the order of alpha-beta's first iteration, and a short restricted alpha-beta search checks the first five of them.
  \item[Elo difference.] For an expected score $S$ per game, $400\log_{10}\bigl(S/(1-S)\bigr)$; RQ1a's interval of $[-10.7, +2.0]$~Elo corresponds to expected scores of 0.485 to 0.503.
  \item[Centipawn.] A hundredth of a pawn, the unit of an engine's evaluation.
  \item[Q and $300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$.] Q is an MCTS value in $[-1, 1]$, the expected game outcome of a move from its player's view; $300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$ is the engine's conversion of it to a centipawn-like score, calibrated to neither Lc0's nor Stockfish's scale.
  \item[Quality-of-service class.] The priority class by which macOS schedules a thread and chooses its type of core.
  \item[vCPU.] A virtual CPU core of a virtual machine.
  \item[Paravirtualised GPU.] A virtual machine's GPU that passes its work to the host's GPU through a driver instead of owning the device.
  \item[Veto.] The arbiter's rule that blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks it clearly below its own move; most override predicates can lift it (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}).
\end{description}

\section{Protocol amendments}
\label{app:amend}
Each cell was first interpreted after its last block (``interpreted'' meaning read for any purpose other than amendment~11's health monitoring): RQ1a on 31 October at 07:50~UTC, RQ2a later that day, RQ2b and RQ5c on 1 November, and RQ1b on 3 November at 18:25, with no interim reading. Every interrupted block resumed from the next unused opening, and the manifests record every invocation. A game whose colour-swapped partner was not played enters no pair and its opening is replayed, except once: before a bookkeeping fix, a watchdog stop split a pair and its opening was skipped, so RQ1a has one pair fewer than planned.

RQ1a's frozen decision rule read ``the GPU search adds strength'' (interval above zero), ``the GPU search costs strength'' (below zero) or ``no detectable effect at the achieved resolution ($\pm X$~Elo)'', and pointed to pre-written framings in the paper blueprint: for a null, ``At the resolution of $\pm X$~Elo, the GPU search adds nothing measurable to a Stockfish-class CPU search on this hardware; the oracle analysis shows why \ldots''; for a loss, ``The hybrid costs $\Delta$ Elo: the two CPU threads surrendered to the GPU path are worth more than the GPU path returns (RQ2 decomposition)''. Amendment~12, merged after the interim reading of RQ1a's first two blocks ($-9.4 \pm 9.1$, committed with amendment~11 about four hours earlier), kept the rule, carried the negative framing's attribution to threads and contention into the protocol, and replaced the null framing's oracle explanation with ``the GPU path does not pay for itself at this budget on this machine''; that clause was written after the interim, no margin of practical relevance or cost was measured, and the data support only that gains above 2.0~Elo are excluded.

Amendment~16 fixed, before RQ6b-R was read, what each class of outcome changes. Besides the health lines of the finished shards (counts of positions judged and of errors; amendment~16), two things had been seen of the oracle by then: the first row of the workstation's stopped run, displayed once on 3 November to check the output's format (amendment~15 (l)), and the row counts and error messages of the shards that failed on the runners' GPU (amendment~15 (i)). Under \emph{little to gain}, the abstract gives the consensus bound and says that an arbiter choosing between the two root candidates could have gained little on these positions, and the conclusion reads its condition on the arbiter as not met by this pair of searches at the budgets tested. Under \emph{room under both judges}, the abstract gives the consensus bound as an upper bound on what any arbiter could gain, not a prediction of game results, and the conclusion reads the condition as open and names an arbiter learned from such positions as the next step. Under \emph{judge-dependent or unresolved}, the abstract and the conclusion give the bounds judge by judge and claim no direction. Under the first two classes, Contribution~4 reports the design and the finding; for \emph{judge-dependent or unresolved}, a class that did not occur, the amendment fixed no wording of Contribution~4, which is noted here after the reading. The class is assigned from the pooled positions and, for any statement about RQ1a, from RQ1a's positions alone, each set reweighted by its own cell's stratum populations; an incomplete run gets a class only if every stratum has a position with verdicts from both judges.

\section{Engine preparation before the freeze}
\label{app:prep}
With the cells' options, the frozen binary's physical footprint after \texttt{isready} is 2.3~GB for AB(8), 4.4~GB for Hybrid(8) and 5.6~GB for MCTS(1), in units of $2^{30}$ bytes (M4). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/footprint_frozen.json (08 §B, M4 frozen build); src/uci/engine.cpp:225; src/uci/uci.cpp:1244-1257

Before the freeze, an exploratory engineering programme (reported in the programme record~\depositcite{}) made four changes, each adopted on the evidence of a gating match. The alpha-beta core was moved from a January 2026 Stockfish development snapshot to Stockfish 19 (+\elo{62.0}{28.2} over the old base at 10+0.1, 136 games); %src: 08 §A sf19port_vs_preport_ab_10p01_v2
the Stockfish thread pool was kept alive across hybrid moves and Stockfish's time manager was given the clock, with the tie-break overrides off (+\elo{74.1}{30.5} over the previous hybrid at 20+0.2, 100 games); %src: 08 §A L7b
alpha-beta was allowed to keep all eight threads with the MCTS worker oversubscribed (+\elo{15.1}{17.0} over the 6+1 split, 300 games); %src: 08 §A L9
and the hybrid's network-output cache was capped at 100{,}000 entries after a 100-game pilot of the main cell read \elo{-41.9}{25.1} for a hybrid process whose physical footprint was 5.8 to 6.1 GB on the 32 GB host (the capped configuration read +\elo{13.9}{30.3} over 101 games). Two null controls before the freeze, in which the hybrid never engaged the transformer, read \elo{-20.9}{28.5} over 100 games at 30+0.3, on the build before the cache cap, and, without preloading the network, \elo{-10.4}{16.8} over 301 games at 10+0.1; they were too small to settle whether the machinery costs strength without the GPU search, and the frozen design has no such control at 30+0.3. %src: 08 §A RQ1a_pilot, L14, L12, L13; 08 §A memory footprint (vmmap, 5.8-6.1 GB)

\section{The arbiter as frozen}
\label{app:ladder}
The verification sub-search of Section~\ref{sec:arbiter} adds a check of 80 to 160~ms for at most four quiet rook or queen checks when a queen is on the board. This appendix lists the decision rules of the frozen engine (source commit \texttt{365f36a}, \texttt{src/hybrid/hybrid\_search.cpp}) under the settings of the hybrid arms. Notation: $R$ is the number of visits the MCTS root received in this move (visits reused from the previous move's tree excluded) and $R_{\mathrm{tot}}$ the number including them; $B$ is the visits of the MCTS move in this move and $S=B/R$; $G$ is the value gap between the MCTS move and the best other move at the root; $c=300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$ is the MCTS move's score in centipawns and $\Delta=c-s_{\mathrm{AB}}$, where $s_{\mathrm{AB}}$ is alpha-beta's score; $Q_g$ is the value gap between the MCTS move and alpha-beta's move in the MCTS statistics. \emph{Sane} means the visit counts are consistent with the playouts and evaluations; \emph{strong} means $R\ge 180$, $B\ge 72$, $S\ge 0.24$, and $B\ge 512$ or $S\ge 0.55$; \emph{clear} means alpha-beta completed depth 10 with $|s_{\mathrm{AB}}|\ge 15$. Every rule also requires a clock-managed search with a positive budget, which holds in all the matches (several names say ``fixed budget'' for historical reasons). Alpha-beta's root scores are in Stockfish's internal units (about three per centipawn) while $s_{\mathrm{AB}}$ and $\Delta$ are in centipawns, so the thresholds mix units. Most rules carry further conditions on the ranks of the two moves in each other's lists and on alpha-beta's effort; the table gives the main ones, and the arbiter reference gives all of them.

The veto holds when alpha-beta has completed depth 10, ranks its own move first and lists the MCTS move at rank 2 or lower among its first 16 root moves (an MCTS move outside that list is never vetoed), and either its average score for the MCTS move is at least 30 internal units below its own move's after at least 10{,}000 nodes on its own move, or it left the MCTS move without an exact score while spending at least ten times as many nodes on its own. The last column gives each rule's relation to the veto: \emph{lifts} (the rule overrides it), \emph{blocked} (the rule cannot fire while it holds), or the stricter condition under which the rule lifts it.
