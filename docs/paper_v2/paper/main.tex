\begin{abstract}
Can a GPU make a Stockfish-class chess engine stronger? We test this for \MF{}, which runs a transformer
Monte Carlo tree search (Lc0's BT4 network at batch size~1) on the GPU of an Apple M2 Max concurrently
with Stockfish~19's alpha-beta search on its CPU, and arbitrates between their moves. Under a protocol
published before the first game, the shipped hybrid played 2{,}002 games against the same binary's
alpha-beta search at the same thread setting (30~s plus 0.3~s per move, unbalanced openings in
colour-swapped pairs). It scored $-4.3$~Elo (95\% CI $-10.7$ to $+2.0$), inconclusive at this sample
size: gains above 2.0~Elo and losses beyond 10.7~Elo are excluded. The transformer was enabled on 53\% of
the hybrid's moves, but the arbiter played the MCTS move on only 7 of 25{,}138 disagreements, so whatever
the GPU search contributed reached the board through the alpha-beta search. At 60~s plus 0.6~s per move
(1{,}000 games, exploratory), with the transformer enabled on 83\% of the moves, the hybrid scored
$-40.5$~Elo (95\% CI $-49.6$ to $-31.4$). In a split configuration that gives the MCTS worker cores of its
own, the split hybrid lost 17.0~Elo to alpha-beta on its six remaining threads. Exploratory cells on
GitHub-hosted virtual M1 machines with three cores, compared only with each other, point the same way.
There the hybrid lost to alpha-beta at the same thread setting by 35.9~Elo at 30+0.3 and by 16.7~Elo at
10+0.1, where the transformer ran on 1.7\% of its moves. Switching off the MCTS search's hints to
alpha-beta and its verification sub-search made no detectable difference, and the split hybrid added
nothing detectable to one alpha-beta thread ($-0.7$~Elo, 95\% CI $-10.9$ to $+9.5$). At matched settings \MF{}'s MCTS was not behind Lc0's
($+47.2$~Elo, $+25.8$ to $+68.6$), and against Stockfish~19 the alpha-beta core scored $+1.4$~Elo ($-7.3$
to $+10.1$), no difference detected. \pending{oracle study RQ6b-R: one sentence with its bound} The ratings
reported in an earlier conference chapter on the system do not hold and are withdrawn; all games, traces,
logs and scripts are released with this paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The strongest chess programs follow one of two designs. One is alpha-beta search with an efficiently updatable neural-network evaluation (NNUE), exemplified by Stockfish~\cite{stockfish,nasu2018,sf12}, which searches tens of millions of nodes per second on a CPU. The other is Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) guided by a deep policy--value network, the approach of AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018} and of Leela Chess Zero (Lc0)~\cite{lc0,monroe2026}, which evaluates far fewer positions (AlphaZero searched about 60{,}000 per second against Stockfish's 60 million~\cite{silver2018}) and compensates with the quality of each evaluation. Both searches can be run on the same position at the same time, and their answers arbitrated, on any machine with a CPU and a GPU; on consumer systems-on-chip such as Apple's M-series~\cite{applem2}, where the CPU and the GPU share one system memory~\cite{appleunified}, this is compact, and contention for the shared resources becomes its main cost. Whether it adds playing strength to a Stockfish-class CPU search is an empirical question, an instance of a general one: how to split test-time compute between a fast search with a cheap evaluation and a slower, more accurate learned evaluator. Here the fast side is Stockfish's alpha-beta search with NNUE, which visits more than ten million positions per second on eight cores of the test chip, and the slow side a transformer that evaluates at most 139 positions per second at batch size~1 on the same chip's GPU (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the question asked below measures one point of that trade-off, in which the slow evaluator is added beside an unchanged fast search rather than given part of its budget. We know of no controlled measurement that answers it for chess.

\ifanon An earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\else Our earlier conference chapter~\cite{niketan2026chapter} described such a system, \MF{}, and reported ratings for its three modes. Re-examination of that chapter\fi{} against the engine's source code and the retained records showed that its central quantitative claims (the ratings, the speed-up and the throughput comparison with Lc0) do not hold: each rating was an average, over opponents, of an assumed opponent rating plus the Elo difference implied by a five-game score; the alpha-beta component is Stockfish's own search; and several implementation statements were wrong (Section~\ref{sec:chapter} lists the main ones\ifanon\else; a Correction has been prepared\fi). This paper is the controlled study the chapter lacked. It takes the engine, updates it as described in Section~\ref{sec:prep}, freezes it, and asks one pre-registered question with everything else held equal:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does running an Lc0-class transformer MCTS (one worker, batch size~1) on the GPU of an Apple M2 Max concurrently with a Stockfish-class alpha-beta search on its CPU, with a final arbiter that chooses between their root moves, change playing strength relative to the alpha-beta search alone, at equal wall-clock and equal CPU-thread budget?}
\end{quote}

Equal CPU-thread budget means the same \texttt{Threads} setting for both arms; the hybrid's single MCTS worker runs as one extra thread, which waits on the GPU during each evaluation and otherwise needs a core like any other thread. ``Lc0-class'' refers to the network, Lc0's BT4 transformer, not to Lc0's search configuration, which batches evaluations over several threads (Section~\ref{sec:system}). The estimand is the score of Hybrid(8) against AB(8) from unbalanced human openings at 30~s plus 0.3~s per move, on one Apple M2 Max under the logged host load, with the shipped hybrid (one MCTS worker at batch size~1) as the treatment. In the frozen engine the GPU search can change the outcome through four channels: the MCTS root hints and the verification sub-search, which can act on any move with the transformer enabled (in RQ1a the hints were present on 84\% of those moves and the verification ran on 18\%); the agreement rule, which can end a move early when the two searches agree; contention for CPU time and memory bandwidth; and the arbiter's override, which never selected the MCTS move in the frozen build's gate L14 (0 of 1{,}344 disagreements) or in the pilot (0 of 1{,}165). RQ1a therefore mostly measures the first three channels, and whether arbitration could be worth anything is the question of the oracle study (RQ6b). %src: results/paper_v2/sprt/L14_hybrid_lean_vs_ab8_30p03/moves.jsonl; results/paper_v2/RQ1a_pilot/moves.jsonl
The answer is measured as an Elo difference with a confidence interval; for the earlier split configuration, the isolation cells of RQ2 price two alpha-beta threads and measure the split hybrid against alpha-beta on the remaining six; and the oracle study, played on the runners (RQ6b-R), is designed to explain it by what the two searches disagree about and who is right when they do. The design was fixed in a protocol merged into the public repository before the first confirmatory game, and every later amendment carries its own merge time (Section~\ref{sec:amend}); the engine was frozen at a tagged commit; every number is traced to a retained artifact, and every table and figure is generated from the artifacts by a script.

\paragraph{Headline result.} Over 2{,}002 games at 30+0.3 (999 colour-swapped pairs and four games
without their partner), the shipped hybrid scored $-4.3$~Elo against AB(8), with a 95\% interval of
$[-10.7, +2.0]$. By the registered rule the result is inconclusive at this $N$: no detectable effect at the achieved
resolution of $\pm 6.3$~Elo, with gains above 2.0~Elo and losses beyond 10.7~Elo excluded. Amendment~12,
merged on 27 October before RQ1a was interpreted on 31 October, reads this outcome as the GPU path not
paying for itself at this budget on this machine, which leaves gains below 2~Elo possible; how RQ1a
combines with the oracle bound was not registered and will be read with RQ6b. The transformer was
enabled on 53\% of the hybrid's moves, and the arbiter played the MCTS move (a descriptive quantity of
RQ6a) on 7 of the 25{,}138 moves on which the two searches disagreed. For the earlier split
configuration, which ran alpha-beta on six of the eight threads while the transformer was enabled, two of
the eight threads were worth 19.8~Elo to plain alpha-beta, and the split hybrid lost a further 17.0~Elo
against alpha-beta on six threads. At 60+0.6, the time control with the most GPU participation (RQ1b,
exploratory, 1{,}000 games), the hybrid scored $-40.5$~Elo against AB(8), with a 95\% interval of
$[-49.6, -31.4]$: the transformer was enabled on 83\% of its moves, the arbiter played the MCTS move on 1
of 17{,}338 disagreements, and the agreement rule, which ends a move early when the two searches agree,
ended 48.6\% of the transformer-enabled moves early (1.0\% at 30+0.3).

\paragraph{The runner cells.} The measurements not yet played when RQ1b finished moved to GitHub-hosted

the hybrid played alpha-beta's move every time. With the hints and the verification sub-search off it
lost by 26.1~Elo (RQ6d-R); over the openings the two cells share, switching that channel off changed the

thread ($-0.7$, $[-10.9, +9.5]$; RQ2b-R), and the direct RQ2c-R agrees with their sum. The arbiter played
the MCTS move on at most 5 of the 9{,}166 to 10{,}081 disagreements of each runner cell at 30+0.3, and on
none of the 661 at 10+0.1. At matched settings (one thread,
minibatch~1, the same network), \MF{}'s MCTS scored $+47.2$~Elo against Lc0 ($[+25.8, +68.6]$; RQ5a-R),
a time-sensitive result that does not separate the searches from their backends' speed.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled methodology, fixed in advance in a time-stamped public protocol, for evaluating hybrid or ensemble chess engines that share one machine: a fair-resource policy, telemetry of when the GPU path is actually enabled, host-load logging with a scheduling gate, component isolation cells and an oracle analysis, combined with the conventions adopted from Fishtest (colour-swapped pairs, unbalanced human openings, pentanomial statistics with normalised Elo), with all artifacts released (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A corrected as-built description of \MF{}, the system first reported in~\cite{niketan2026chapter}: a concurrent alpha-beta plus MCTS runtime with shared root state, MCTS-to-alpha-beta root-ordering hints and a gated arbiter, with the BT4 transformer on Metal Performance Shaders Graph; the new element is the measurement of its batch-1 latency ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:system}) and of the contention between the two searches inside games (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Measurements for the research questions of Section~\ref{sec:rqs}: the marginal value of the GPU search at an equal \texttt{Threads} setting at two time controls on the M2 Max and at two on three-core runners, the effect of the channel formed by the MCTS hints and the verification sub-search, and, for the split configuration, the value of the alpha-beta threads it gives up and what the MCTS worker returns for them (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the parity of the alpha-beta core with Stockfish 19 and the MCTS search's standing against Lc0 at matched settings are checked as validity conditions.
  \item The design of an oracle analysis that bounds, under two stated judges, what any arbiter choosing between the two root candidates at decision time could gain from this pair of searches, and measures what the implemented arbiter achieves (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}); the arbitration telemetry of every hybrid move is reported here, with \pending{the oracle study's results on the runners (RQ6b-R)}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Alpha-beta engines}
Stockfish combines alpha-beta pruning~\cite{knuth1975} with aspiration windows, null-move and futility pruning, late-move reductions and a shared transposition table, and since 2020 it has evaluated positions with an NNUE network~\cite{nasu2018,sf12}, whose first layer is updated incrementally as moves are made and unmade. Its development is driven by Fishtest, a distributed testing framework whose statistical conventions (game pairs with colour swap, unbalanced opening books, pentanomial statistics, sequential tests) are designed to measure small strength differences~\cite{fishtest,stockfish,sfbooks}. The two designs are not independent in what they know: Stockfish's networks have been trained partly on positions evaluated by Leela Chess Zero since Stockfish 14~\cite{sf14}, and all of Stockfish 19's training positions were rescored with a strong Leela network~\cite{stockfish}.

\subsection{Neural-network MCTS engines}
MCTS~\cite{coulom2006,kocsis2006,browne2012} with PUCT selection~\cite{rosin2011} and a policy--value network, as in AlphaZero~\cite{silver2018}, grows a search tree whose leaves are evaluated by the network, so its quality depends on how many evaluations fit into the time for a move. Lc0 is an open-source engine in the AlphaZero tradition whose current networks are transformers~\cite{monroe2026,lc0}; the BT4 network used here is one of them. Transformers have also been trained to play chess without explicit search~\cite{ruoss2024}. On a given GPU the evaluation rate is governed by batching: at small batch sizes, fixed framework overhead dominates inference time~\cite{fernandez2023}; Section~\ref{sec:system} measures latency against batch size for the backend used here, without attributing it to its components. Batched MCTS keeps its tree statistics usable by marking in-flight nodes with a virtual loss~\cite{chaslot2008}, or by correcting the selection rule for the simulations still in flight~\cite{liu2020wuuct}. Co-executing one workload on the CPU and the GPU of a unified-memory Apple chip helps in some phases of language-model inference and not in others~\cite{mohite2026}.

\subsection{Combining minimax and MCTS}
The algorithmic literature on hybrids embeds one search inside the other. Baier and Winands embed shallow minimax searches in the selection, rollout and backpropagation phases of MCTS~\cite{baier2013,baier2015} and later add static state evaluations~\cite{baier2018}; Lanctot et al.\ back up an implicit minimax value alongside the Monte Carlo average~\cite{lanctot2014}; Huang shows that alpha-beta and MT-SSS* can be expressed as rollout algorithms~\cite{huang2015}, and Baier unifies MCTS and alpha-beta in one rollout-based algorithm~\cite{baier2017}. Ji and Thielscher vary the depth of the embedded minimax search dynamically~\cite{ji2023}. A common motivation for these hybrids is the weakness of sampling-based search in tactical positions: UCT can miss shallow traps that minimax finds~\cite{ramanujan2010}, and in synthetic chess-like games it can show lookahead pathology, although minimax is similarly susceptible in that setting~\cite{nguyen2024}. In the AlphaZero setting, Chen et al.\ add an alpha-beta phase to an AlphaZero-style player~\cite{chen2019}, Helfenstein et al.\ combine a mixture of experts with MCTS in chess~\cite{helfenstein2024}, and Cohen-Solal and Cazenave show that minimax-based learning remains competitive with MCTS-based learning~\cite{cohen2023}.

\subsection{Engines that combine or compare the paradigms}
Engine practice has its own precedents. Scorpio offers MCTS with alpha-beta rollouts and, with neural networks, a network-guided MCTS that uses alpha-beta searches as a prior~\cite{scorpio}; Komodo, since version 12, has an MCTS mode whose tree is expanded best-first from tiny alpha-beta searches instead of random playouts or a network~\cite{komodo}; a 2017 Stockfish branch experimented with a Monte Carlo search on top of Stockfish's evaluation~\cite{sfmcts2017}; the lc0-stockfish-hybrid project uses Stockfish as a filter inside Lc0's selection~\cite{lc0sfhybrid}; Ceres and Allie are independent MCTS searches on Lc0 networks~\cite{ceres,allie}; Stoofvlees II, an MCTS engine whose design departs from the AlphaZero recipe, won the 2023 World Computer Chess Championship~\cite{pascutto2025}; and ShashChess has an experimental option that applies Monte Carlo tree search, with its own threads, in selected position types~\cite{shashchess}. Adjacent work models human play across skill levels with a neural policy~\cite{maia2} and combines such policies with engine search~\cite{carlson2026}, for goals other than playing strength.

\subsection{Choosing among several programs' moves}
Selecting a move from the proposals of several programs predates neural networks. Alth\"ofer's multiple-choice systems give a human decider the candidate moves of two or more programs~\cite{althofer2003,althofer2004}; Clever~\&~Smart, an automatic version in which two versions of Shredder passed their proposals and search data to a control program that chose the move and managed the clock, finished second at the 1998 International Paderborn Computer Chess Championship~\cite{cleversmart}. In computer shogi, consultation by majority vote among several programs~\cite{obata2011} and an optimistic rule that plays the move of the program reporting the best evaluation~\cite{sugiyama2011} were compared in controlled matches, and consultation was used in the distributed system Akara 2010~\cite{hoki2013}. In computer Go, a team of diverse, individually weaker programs can outplay a uniform team of stronger ones~\cite{marcolino2013,marcolino2014}, which suggests that the positions where searches of different paradigms disagree are where an arbiter could gain, and how much is what the oracle study is designed to measure; and root parallelisation combines independent MCTS searches at the root~\cite{chaslot2008}. The hybrid studied here is a two-member consultation between searches of different paradigms, run concurrently on one chip under an equal \texttt{Threads} setting; with two members a majority vote cannot break a disagreement, and the arbiter resolves it by defaulting to the alpha-beta move unless gated predicates favour the MCTS move, which is closer to a veto rule than to the optimistic rule. What is new is not the consultation but a controlled, equal-budget measurement of what the consulting search contributes, including through the alpha-beta search it accompanies, together with the design of a judge-based bound on what any such arbiter could gain.

\subsection{Measuring strength}
Elo differences between near-equal engines are small relative to game-to-game noise, and the measurement conventions matter as much as the games. Fishtest's methodology, documented by Van den Bergh~\cite{vdbergh_gsprt,vdbergh_rw,vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}, plays colour-swapped game pairs from unbalanced human openings (UHO)~\cite{sfbooks,pohl2024} so that draws do not swamp the signal, and reports pentanomial statistics and normalised Elo; the book choice itself is debated~\cite{uhocritique}. Draw modelling in Elo~\cite{szczecinski2020} and uncertainty estimation in paired-comparison experiments~\cite{glickman1999} are the statistical background; Bayesian Elo~\cite{bayeselo} and Ordo~\cite{ordo} fit static paired-comparison models to multi-engine results (Ordo is the tool used here), and whole-history rating~\cite{coulom2008} extends such models to players whose strength changes over time. Ratings from public lists such as CCRL are specific to their reference hardware and time control~\cite{ccrl} and cannot be imported to another machine; in its seventeenth season the Top Chess Engine Championship gave CPU and GPU engines separate lower leagues, each on its own hardware, and let the two groups meet only at a later stage~\cite{haworth2020}. Test-position suites, mostly tactical (the Strategic Test Suite is not)~\cite{bratko1982,sts,eret,arasan,lichesspuzzles}, measure something other than playing strength and, for an engine that was tuned on them, measure it optimistically.

\section{The system as built}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Alpha-beta component}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish's search and NNUE evaluation. At the freeze it is a port of Stockfish 19 with its SFNNv16 network (\texttt{nn-1a298aa575a0.nnue}, 98.5 MB); the engine's \texttt{bench} command reproduces the upstream signature of 2{,}497{,}913 nodes, so the search is node-for-node Stockfish's. %src: results/paper_v2/queue_paper_log.txt (bench lines 25, 60, 98, 105); upstream tag sf_19; 03 §0
Its local changes are few: the root accepts move-ordering hints from the MCTS component, the previous principal variation is kept after an unproven fail-high, the hybrid can set the size of the thread pool, and there are an AArch64 spin-wait hint, two branch-likelihood hints, thread quality-of-service classes, a de-duplication of \texttt{searchmoves}, a transposition-table allocation check and a NEON kernel. There is no GPU code in the alpha-beta search; NNUE runs on the CPU by design, because incremental CPU evaluation is faster than any GPU round trip at the granularity of a node. On the test machine the frozen binary's search reaches 1.47 million nodes/s on one thread, 12.8 million on eight threads and 15.1 million on all twelve cores at fixed depth (Figure~\ref{fig:m2}; measured after the cells). %src: results/paper_v2/micro/ab_scaling_frozen.json (depth 16, median of 5; 08 §B M2 frozen build)

\subsection{MCTS component and neural-network backend}
The MCTS component follows Lc0's search: PUCT selection with first-play urgency, a policy softmax temperature, tree reuse between moves and a direct-mapped cache of network outputs keyed on the position; it implements Lc0's algorithm and parameters and reads Lc0's network format, and is not claimed as a contribution. Nodes are 128 bytes and aligned to the M-series cache line; a 4-byte \texttt{os\_unfair\_lock} protects score updates. The BT4 transformer (\texttt{BT4-1024x15x32h}, 15 encoder blocks, 32 heads, 1024-wide; 383 MB of weights) is built once as a Metal Performance Shaders graph~\cite{mpsgraph} with an FP16 body and FP32 inputs and outputs, and encoded into a command buffer for each evaluation; one evaluation is in flight at a time. The stand-alone MCTS mode runs one worker at batch size 1; the hybrid runs one worker.

Batch-1 inference is the structural ceiling of this design. With the frozen binary, measured after the
cells (1 November 2026, with outside processes using 1.5 to 3.6 cores during the run), a single BT4
evaluation takes 7.21~ms in FP16 (139 positions/s), the precision the engine uses, and 8.56~ms in FP32 (medians of 40 timed iterations after five warm-up iterations);
a batch of 8 takes 18.9~ms (422 positions/s) and a batch of 64 takes 119~ms (538 positions/s), so
batching could raise throughput almost fourfold (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). The engine as shipped does not
batch; whether batching, with virtual loss or in-flight counts keeping the tree statistics usable~\cite{chaslot2008,liu2020wuuct}, would
help it inside a game is not tested here (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).
%src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency_frozen.json (08 §B, M1 frozen build); src/uci/engine.cpp:140 (NNMetalFP16 true)
A search with Lc0 0.32.1's Metal backend and the same weights reaches 217 nodes/s at minibatch 1 and at best 763 nodes/s (minibatch 32) in the same run; Lc0's raw-evaluation benchmark failed on this machine (``Failed to import MPS module''), so no raw Lc0 latency was measured. \MF{}'s evaluation rate is therefore below Lc0's search rate at batch size 1, the setting its search uses (139 against 217 per second), and at each program's best batch size (538 against 763 per second); at batch sizes 2 and 4 it is above, and from 8 to 64 below (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). The two tools count different things (network evaluations against search nodes), so these comparisons are indicative only. %src: results/paper_v2/micro/nn_latency_frozen.json (lc0 nps_steady_median; 08 §B M1 frozen build)
Inside a game the MCTS worker competes with eight alpha-beta threads for memory bandwidth and scheduling; its observed rate is reported with the game telemetry in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Hybrid runtime and arbiter}
\label{sec:arbiter}
On every move the coordinator starts the alpha-beta search and the MCTS search on the same position. Alpha-beta keeps all $T$ threads and the single MCTS worker runs as an extra thread that blocks on the GPU during each evaluation (\texttt{HybridOversubscribe}). Stockfish's clock-based time manager owns the move (\texttt{HybridFollowABTime}): the coordinator ends the move as soon as the alpha-beta search returns and stops the MCTS search, which is otherwise limited by the coordinator's own per-move budget (the remaining clock divided by 30 plus three quarters of the increment, within fixed bounds) less the move overhead. The two searches publish their root state through two cache-line-aligned structures whose fields are written with relaxed atomic stores and made visible through a release-ordered flag and counter; the decision reads them after both searches have stopped, which each thread signals with a release-ordered completion flag. If a search has not stopped within a grace period (a quarter of the budget, between 1 and 5~s), the coordinator decides without the MCTS statistics; in the gating match L14 this never happened. %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 468-500, 2849-2855, 4037-4042, 4124-4141; hybrid_search.h 36-141; L14 engines.log.gz ("subsearch stop grace expired": 0)

At the start of a move the alpha-beta side reads up to eight moves from the MCTS root, which at that moment holds the subtree kept from the previous move, or nothing. These hints only reorder alpha-beta's initial list of root moves, so every thread searches the first hint first in the first iteration; from the second iteration Stockfish orders the root by score as usual, the aspiration windows are unaffected, and nothing else in the search reads the hints. When the coordinator's budget is at least one second, the first five hints are first verified by a restricted alpha-beta search of $\min(240~\text{ms}, \text{budget}/8)$; with a queen on the board, a further check of 80 to 160~ms can follow for each verified candidate that is a quiet rook or queen check (at most four). The verification shares the transposition and history tables with the main search, and its result feeds two of the predicates below. Its time is spent from the game clock before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move, and it replaces state that the time manager carries from one search to the next, so the hybrid's alpha-beta search does not divide the clock exactly as the alpha-beta arm does (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). %src: 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 572-597, 3102-3176, 3537-3669, 3829-3864; search/search.cpp 59-98, 275-277

When both searches name the same move, it is played, except that two tie-break rules can play a third move from the MCTS list when its prior or its value strongly favours it. When they disagree, the arbiter plays alpha-beta's move unless a predicate selects the MCTS move: 36 predicates are tested in a fixed order, and 31 of them can fire under the tested settings (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}). A veto blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks its own move first and the MCTS move lower, and either its average score for the MCTS move trails by at least 30 internal units with at least 10{,}000 nodes spent on its own move, or it barely searched the MCTS move. Of the 31 predicates, 24 override the veto, two do so only under stricter conditions, four are blocked by it, and one applies only when alpha-beta has not reached depth 10, when there is no veto. The veto encodes a design assumption: with a few hundred MCTS visits against millions of alpha-beta nodes, a move that alpha-beta has searched to depth 10 and found worse by a margin is more often a tactical oversight of the shallow MCTS search than a long-term judgement that alpha-beta misses. The oracle study is designed to test this assumption on the disagreements (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}). The pawn-lever and quiet-attack tie-break overrides, which were added after the chapter, are off. In the frozen build's gate L14 the arbiter never played the MCTS move (0 of 1{,}344 disagreements), nor in the pilot (0 of 1{,}165); in RQ1a it played it on 7 of 25{,}138 (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}). A time-safety rule disables the transformer for a move when the estimated budget is below 400~ms or the remaining clock below 3~s; the move is then plain alpha-beta with Stockfish's own output, and the engine logs the rule but writes no decision trace. The position classifier that the chapter described as weighting the decision can only lengthen the coordinator's budget, never alpha-beta's search time; a longer budget lengthens the MCTS search, the verification cap and the grace period. An agreement rule ends a move early when both searches have named the same move in three consecutive polls after a quarter of the budget, with at least 100 MCTS nodes and alpha-beta at depth 10; it ended 660 of the 68{,}456 transformer-enabled moves of RQ1a early (1.0\%) and 6{,}485 of the 38{,}645 of the split hybrid in RQ2b (16.8\%), whose MCTS search ran a median of 70 evaluations per transformer-enabled move against 36 in RQ1a (Section~\ref{sec:results}); its time saving was not measured directly. %src: results/paper_v2/{RQ1a,RQ2b}/engines.log ("engines agree, stopping early"; tables/flags.md) and summary.json moves_hybrid (08 §E Final analyses); 365f36a hybrid_search.cpp 1860-1873, 4058-4082, 4946-4951, 5288-5306, 5366-5480

With \texttt{HybridTrace} on, as in every hybrid arm, the coordinator writes one decision line per move after both searches have stopped and before \texttt{bestmove}, so the trace cannot affect either search; its cost counts towards the move's time and was not measured. The line records the reason, both candidates, their scores and visit counts, every predicate's value, the MCTS top ten and alpha-beta's top sixteen root moves; the recorded reason is the first predicate in ladder order that holds, which need not be the one that lifted the veto. The score the hybrid reports before \texttt{bestmove} is alpha-beta's (Stockfish's normalised centipawns, with mates as $\pm(30000-\text{plies})$ centipawns) unless it plays the MCTS move, when it reports $300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$, bounded at $\pm 1140$; time-safety moves report Stockfish's own output. At the first \texttt{isready} the engine loads the network, builds the Metal graph and runs one batch-1 warm-up evaluation before it answers \texttt{readyok}, which the match manager awaits before the game starts, so the game clock does not pay for initialisation. \section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Principles and fair-resource policy}
The alpha-beta component is Stockfish, so its strength is not a contribution and is never compared across machines. Every primary comparison is \emph{same binary, same machine, same wall-clock, same \texttt{Threads} setting}, with the GPU search switched on or off; the \texttt{Threads} setting is a nominal thread budget, because the hybrid runs its MCTS worker as one extra thread (amendment~3). How much CPU time that thread takes was not measured; its in-game evaluation rate, which halves when alpha-beta holds all eight threads (Section~\ref{sec:results}), is consistent with its competing with alpha-beta for cores or memory bandwidth. Alpha-beta was not also run with more threads to take the cores that thread uses, and the protocol's thread-scaling cells were not run. The protocol~\depositcite{} fixed the hypotheses, cells, sample sizes, statistics and decision rules before data collection: its frozen version (SHA-256 of the protocol file: \texttt{03020292ae3441df} \texttt{5a0eb2eab1e67403} \texttt{a0722a7ee85fcb59} \texttt{0dfac04e852a89f2}) was merged into the public repository on 14 October 2026 at 06:46~UTC\ifanon\else{} (pull request~\#77, a server-side time stamp)\fi, and the first confirmatory game started at 08:09~UTC. The registration is the author's own, in a public repository with server-side time stamps, not an entry in a third-party registry. It was amended only by numbered entries written before the affected cell was interpreted (fifteen amendments, each with its merge time; Section~\ref{sec:amend}). All games, the engine logs of the cells with a hybrid arm, traces, manifests with hashes and seeds, and thermal logs are retained, and every figure and table is generated from them by a script.

\subsection{Match protocol}
\label{sec:match}
Each cell is a fixed number of game pairs: every opening is played twice with colours swapped. Openings are drawn in a seeded random order from Fishtest's \texttt{UHO\_Lichess\_4852\_v1} book of 2{,}632{,}036 unbalanced human positions~\cite{sfbooks}; the same seed is used in every cell of a research question, so arms and cells see the same opening sample. Engines are restarted for every game and do not ponder; the \MF{} arms use a 50 ms move overhead (the protocol's setting after a 12 ms overrun at 10+0.1 with the 10 ms default), and the reference engines keep their defaults (10 ms for Stockfish 19). Games are adjudicated as drawn after move 34 when both engines' scores have stayed within $\pm 20$ cp for eight consecutive moves each (Fishtest's draw rule) and as lost after three consecutive scores beyond $\pm 600$ cp from both engines' perspectives, and they are capped at 200 moves; adjudicated games, time forfeits and any other terminations, such as crashes, are counted per cell (no game ended in a crash), and no game is excluded after the fact. The rules read the last score each engine reports before its move; for the hybrid this is alpha-beta's score except on the rare moves where it plays the MCTS move (Section~\ref{sec:arbiter}). Each cell is split into four blocks of equal size that draw disjoint openings; the blocks of the four primary cells are interleaved round-robin over the calendar so that thermal and background drift are balanced across them, and later cells run block after block; the thermal state is logged every minute. The hybrid's telemetry (\texttt{HybridTrace}) records, for every move, whether the transformer was active, the MCTS playouts and evaluations, both candidates, and the arbiter's decision and reason.

\subsection{Statistics}
The primary estimator is the pentanomial model: each game pair scores 0, $\tfrac12$, 1, $1\tfrac12$ or 2 points, and the mean and variance of the pair score give the logistic Elo difference with a 95\% normal-approximation confidence interval, the likelihood of superiority (LOS), and normalised Elo (nElo), which divides the score excess by its per-game standard deviation, so that the number of games needed to resolve a difference does not depend on the draw rate or the opening book~\cite{vdbergh_nelo,fishtest}. The statistics are computed independently from the PGN by our own script and must agree with fastchess's report to rounding. Multi-engine tables use Ordo~\cite{ordo} anchored to Stockfish 19 at 0 on this hardware (none is part of this version); no absolute rating is claimed. The oracle study's intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples games as clusters within strata (10{,}000 resamples), and its rates over all disagreements are reweighted by the number of disagreements in each stratum (game phase by MCTS visit count, amendment~6), which will be reported with it. The protocol fixed no smallest effect of interest: its rule reports a null result as no detectable effect at the achieved resolution, the half-width of the 95\% interval. Block consistency is tested with Cochran's $Q$ over the four block estimates, with a block bootstrap as a robustness interval that resamples the runner's chunks, the contiguous sessions of play between scheduling pauses. The derived estimate of RQ2c (RQ2b plus RQ2a) comes from cells that share their openings, so its variance includes the covariance of the two cells' per-opening pair scores. Besides the exclusion analysis of amendment~8, every cell is reported with a pair-level regression of the pair score on the fraction of its minutes under high outside load (giving the estimate at zero load), with the pairs that contain a time forfeit removed, and with forfeits scored as draws; RQ1a is also reported without its first 92 openings, which the pilot and the set-aside games had already played (amendment~12). A result that moves by more than one standard error under any of these is called load-sensitive. Every cell is also reported by the macOS build on which each of its pairs started (amendment~13; for RQ1a this breakdown was added after its interpretation). RQ1a at 30+0.3 is the single confirmatory test; all other cells are estimates with confidence intervals and are labelled exploratory, no family-wise correction is applied, and every planned cell is reported whether or not it ran to completion.

\subsection{Cells on GitHub-hosted runners}
\label{sec:runners}
On 4 November 2026 the author moved every measurement that had not yet been played from the workstation to GitHub-hosted macOS runners, as many at a time as the account allows (amendment~15). A runner is a virtual machine on an Apple M1 (\texttt{VirtualMac2,1}) with three CPU cores, 7~GB of memory and a paravirtualised GPU, running macOS~26. In the probe that fixed the runner settings, the BT4 network took 21.35 and 19.65~ms per evaluation at batch size~1 in FP16 on two runners, against 7.6~ms on the M2 Max, and alpha-beta searched 1.44 and 1.27 million nodes per second on three threads. The frozen source was built once on a runner from the freeze tag, with its Homebrew libraries bundled and its bench signature checked, and that one binary played every block of every runner cell. A runner cell keeps every registered setting except three. $T = 3$, the runner's core count, wherever a workstation cell uses $T = 8$; so the split hybrid is one alpha-beta thread beside the MCTS worker, and the shipped hybrid's extra worker has no spare core. Each engine gets a hash of 1{,}024~MB, so that two \MF{} processes fit in the runner's memory. And a cell is played in blocks of 50 pairs (100 for RQ1c-R), one runner per block, with the blocks of compared cells dispatched together, so that differences between runners fall on both arms alike. The runner cells are RQ1a-R (Hybrid(3) against AB(3)), RQ6d-R (the same without the hints and the verification), RQ2a-R (AB(1) against AB(3)), RQ2b-R (Hybrid-split(3) against AB(1)) and RQ2c-R (Hybrid-split(3) against AB(3)), each 500 pairs at 30+0.3 over the same openings, the first 500 of RQ1a's order; RQ1c-R, Hybrid(3) against AB(3) at 10+0.1 over 1{,}000 pairs; RQ5a-R, MCTS(1) against Lc0-mb1 over 300 pairs, with Lc0 given the same 50~ms move overhead; the oracle study RQ6b-R; and the microbenchmarks M1 and M2. A runner runs nothing besides its one measurement, so the host-load gate and analyses do not apply. What other virtual machines on the same physical host do cannot be observed; the block estimates of each cell, with Cochran's $Q$, show the heterogeneity it causes. The jobs' logs are public, so a block printed only its health (games, time forfeits, clock overruns without a side, GPU activity and GPU errors), and each runner cell was read once, after its last block. Runner cells are exploratory and are compared only with each other; the workstation results stand.

Two problems surfaced on the runners and were dealt with before any runner cell or oracle shard was read. The first three blocks lost 7 of their 300 games on time, with moves arriving up to 217~ms after the clock ran out. This is the virtual machine's timing jitter, which a larger move overhead could not absorb without changing the time control. Fastchess therefore forgives an overrun of up to one second in every runner cell (amendment~15 (j)); the engines are not told, and those three blocks are a pilot that enters no estimate. Each runner cell is reported as played and under the registered rule without the margin, rebuilt from the clocks recorded in the games, with every game that had an overrun lost by the engine that overran first. When the two estimates differ by more than the primary standard error, the cell is called time-sensitive. The second problem was the oracle's Lc0 judge, which failed on the runners' GPU: during its long searches the device reported hangs, and Metal Performance Shaders stopped Lc0 with a failed assertion, on 110 of the first 116 positions. Every Lc0 judgment made on a runner's GPU was discarded. A judgment that ends in an error, overruns its time limit or coincides with a GPU error is now judged again in a fresh process, and the Lc0 judge runs on the CPU with Lc0's CPU defaults (amendment~15 (i) and (k)); Stockfish~19 judges with three threads.

RQ1a, the confirmatory cell, ended at 2{,}002 games: 999 pairs and four unpaired games (one pair was lost
to a runner fault that was fixed before any cell was interpreted; Section~\ref{sec:amend}). The hybrid
scored $-4.3$~Elo against AB(8), with a 95\% interval of $[-10.7, +2.0]$ (nElo $-10.5 \pm 15.2$;
Table~\ref{tab:cells}, Figure~\ref{fig:forest}). The interval spans zero, so by the registered rule the result is inconclusive at
this $N$: no effect of the concurrent GPU search was detected at an equal \texttt{Threads} setting at the
achieved resolution of $\pm 6.3$~Elo, and the experiment excludes a gain above 2.0~Elo and a loss beyond
10.7~Elo. Amendment~12 reads this as the GPU path not paying for itself at this budget on this machine;
gains below 2~Elo are not excluded.

(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The load log began during block~0, so 205 of its 250 pairs have no load
samples; the exclusion analysis keeps them and the regression leaves them out (794 of 999 pairs); every
later block, and every block of the other cells but one RQ2b pair, is covered. In detail, at zero outside load the pair-level regression gives $-3.0 \pm 8.0$, the

\subsection{RQ2: thread cost versus GPU value}
RQ2a measures what two of the eight alpha-beta threads are worth to plain alpha-beta, the threads the
pre-amendment split gave to the GPU side on its transformer-enabled moves. AB(6) scored $-19.8$~Elo against AB(8)
over 500 pairs, with a 95\% interval of $[-28.6, -11.1]$ (Table~\ref{tab:cells}): at 30+0.3 on this
machine two of the eight alpha-beta threads are worth about 20~Elo. By the registered criterion the cell
is load-sensitive (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}): the pair-level regression puts the estimate at zero outside
load at $-24.7 \pm 9.7$, 4.9~Elo from the primary estimate (standard error 4.5), and the low-load pairs
alone give $-23.8 \pm 10.1$. The regression's positive slope says that the gap between six and eight
threads was smaller in pairs played under outside load, as expected when other processes take cores that
only the eight-thread arm would have used; both estimates are therefore reported. The four blocks read
$-9.7$, $-32.1$, $-23.7$ and $-13.9$~Elo (Cochran's $Q$ = 3.69 on 3 degrees of freedom, $p = 0.30$), and
the bootstrap over the runner's 113 chunks gives $[-31.4, -11.7]$. The first block was played before the
load gate existed and had the most outside load (52\% of its pairs overlapped a minute above three cores,
against 8--38\% in the later blocks); it is also the only block on the first macOS build, so the
difference between builds ($-15.3$, standard error 10.2; $-9.7 \pm 17.0$ over 125 pairs on the beta against
$-25.1 \pm 10.6$ over 347 on the release candidate, the 28 pairs on the final release being too few for
an estimate) cannot be separated from load and scheduling.

RQ2b compares the split hybrid, as built, with AB(6). On its transformer-enabled moves (59\% of its moves)
the split hybrid's alpha-beta side had the same six threads as its opponent, with the MCTS worker on the
rest of the eight-thread budget and the GPU; on its time-safety moves it played plain alpha-beta on all
eight threads, because the time-safety rule restores the full pool, and it switched to them once in 818 of
its 1{,}000 games and never back, each change rebuilding alpha-beta's thread pool with empty per-thread
histories. It scored $-17.0$~Elo against AB(6) over 500 pairs, with a 95\% interval of $[-26.2, -7.9]$:
with at least as many alpha-beta threads as its opponent on every move, the split configuration lost
strength. It never played an MCTS move against its alpha-beta side's choice: the two searches disagreed
on 34.0\% of the traced moves, and the MCTS move was chosen on none of these 13{,}152 disagreements, so the
deficit arose in the alpha-beta side's search inside the hybrid rather than in moves chosen by the GPU
search. The cell is not load-sensitive: the estimate at zero outside load is $-16.3 \pm 10.1$ and the
low-load pairs alone give $-16.6 \pm 10.3$, both within 0.7~Elo of the primary estimate (standard error
4.7). The four blocks read $-27.9$, $-12.5$, $-12.5$ and $-15.3$~Elo (Cochran's $Q$ = 1.85, $p = 0.60$),
and the bootstrap over 120 chunks gives $[-26.5, -7.6]$. The first block, the only one on the macOS beta,
also had the most outside load (54\% of its pairs overlapped a minute above three cores, against 2--18\%
later) and the lowest MCTS evaluation rate (a median of 89.5 evaluations per second of move time, against
118.8--129.7 in the later blocks), so the difference between the release candidate and the beta ($+15.3$,
standard error 11.4; the builds read $-27.9 \pm 18.4$, $-12.5 \pm 12.7$ and $-15.3 \pm 18.5$ for the beta, the
release candidate and the final release, over 125, 250 and 125 pairs) cannot be attributed to the build.

The logs show how the split hybrid's alpha-beta search differed from AB(6)'s, without separating the
causes. The GPU side reaches that search through the root hints, present on 91\% of its
transformer-enabled moves, and the verification sub-search, which ran on 30\% of them; through the CPU,
memory and clock that the two sides share; and through the agreement rule, which ended 6{,}485 of the
38{,}645 transformer-enabled moves early (16.8\%, against 1.0\% for the shipped hybrid of RQ1a, whose MCTS
search ran half as many evaluations per enabled move: a median of 36 against 70, where the rule requires
100 nodes). The split configuration also rebuilt alpha-beta's thread pool, with empty per-thread
histories, when it switched to time-safety moves, which the shipped hybrid never does. The split hybrid
divided its clock differently from AB(6): in its first ten moves it spent 0.96~s per move against
1.27~s, and more than AB(6) in moves 21 to 40 (Table~\ref{tab:timeprofile}), whereas the shipped hybrid and
AB(8) used their clocks alike in RQ1a. RQ6d, which switches the hints and the verification off, was played
only on the runners, and with the shipped hybrid rather than the split (Section~\ref{sec:runner-results});
no cell switches the agreement rule off, so which of these channels carries the deficit is not
established here.

Derived as RQ2b plus RQ2a, with the covariance of the shared openings, RQ2c puts the split hybrid at
$-36.9$~Elo against AB(8) (95\% interval $[-49.6, -24.2]$). The registered interpretation singled out one
pattern, a positive RQ2b with a negative RQ2c, which would have meant that the GPU search helps only when
CPU threads are free; RQ2b is negative, so that pattern did not occur. The shipped configuration of RQ1a,
which keeps all eight alpha-beta threads and runs the MCTS worker as an extra thread, stayed within
$[-10.7, +2.0]$~Elo of AB(8), an interval that does not overlap the split's (an across-match
comparison), and no configuration measured here showed a gain. On the runners, at $T = 3$, the direct cell
was played and agreed with the derived estimate (Section~\ref{sec:runner-results}).

\subsection{RQ5: component parity}
RQ5c checks the baseline. Against Stockfish~19 with the same eight threads and hash, the frozen build's
alpha-beta engine scored $+1.4$~Elo over 500 pairs, with a 95\% interval of $[-7.3, +10.1]$
(Table~\ref{tab:cells}). By the registered reading this is a parity interval: no difference was
detected and differences outside the interval are excluded, but no equivalence margin was set, so the
result does not show that the two engines are equally strong. The AB(8) baseline of RQ1a and RQ2a is
therefore within this range of Stockfish~19. The cell is not load-sensitive: the estimate at zero
outside load is $+0.3 \pm 9.5$ and the low-load pairs alone give $-0.4 \pm 9.6$, both within 1.8~Elo of
the primary estimate (standard error 4.4). The four blocks read $+2.8$, $+6.9$, $-2.8$ and $-1.4$~Elo
(Cochran's $Q$ = 0.73, $p = 0.87$), and the bootstrap over 113 chunks gives $[-8.1, +11.2]$; the macOS
builds read $+2.8 \pm 17.7$ (beta), $+2.1 \pm 12.4$ (release candidate) and $-1.4 \pm 16.6$ (final release),
over 125, 250 and 125 pairs, and no difference between them exceeds its standard error. The arms differ in two respects: the
\MF{} arm uses the 50~ms move overhead of every \MF{} arm and Stockfish keeps its 10~ms default
(Section~\ref{sec:match}), which leaves the alpha-beta arm slightly less time per move, and Stockfish's
PGO build searched about 5\% more nodes per second (a median of 11.5 against 11.0 million;
Table~\ref{tab:timing}). RQ5a, the MCTS search against Lc0 at matched settings, was played on the runners
(Section~\ref{sec:runner-results}).

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Two platforms and what generalises.} The workstation cells come from one M2 Max and the runner cells from virtual M1 machines with three cores, and the CUDA build of the same engine is out of scope. The equal-budget accounting, the oracle method and the way the channels through which the GPU search affects the move are measured carry over to other hardware; the absolute Elo, the frequencies measured here, the M2 Max and batch-1 inference do not. The engine's Core ML path to the Neural Engine is off by default and was not tested. A later Apple chip with a larger GPU, or a discrete GPU with batched inference, would need the transformer-enabled fraction, the in-game evaluation rate and RQ1 measured again. \textbf{An under-provisioned GPU arm.} GPU MCTS is normally run with several workers and batched, overlapping evaluations; the arm studied here runs one worker at batch size~1 with one evaluation in flight, and only on the moves where the transformer is enabled, so the in-game evaluations per move are reported per cell next to the rates this chip reaches with batching (Figure~\ref{fig:m1}). A null result would not show that a better-driven GPU search cannot help. \textbf{Opening book.} UHO openings make differences measurable but exaggerate Elo spreads relative to balanced books; nElo is reported for that reason and the book critique applies~\cite{uhocritique}. \textbf{Transformer-enabled fraction.} The shipped time-safety rule is part of the system under test; at 30+0.3 the transformer is enabled on about half of the hybrid's moves, and at 10+0.1, where the move budget is mostly below 400 ms, on 1.7\% (RQ1c-R, on the runners), so results at short time controls measure the hybrid's alpha-beta search with the rest of the hybrid running around it. The fraction is reported descriptively per cell; how the effect depends on GPU participation is read from the time-control cells, not from variation within a cell, and no statement is made beyond the time controls tested. \textbf{Tuning leakage.} The hybrid and MCTS heuristics were tuned on Bratko--Kopec and Lichess puzzle samples; any suite result for \MF{} is optimistic and is labelled so; game results are unaffected. \textbf{Reference drift.} Stockfish 19 is the alpha-beta base and the parity reference; the gap to the chapter-era base is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:prep}. \textbf{Adjudication.} The draw and resignation rules end a game only when both engines' own scores agree that it is decided; the 200-move cap ends a game regardless of the scores, and capped games are included in the adjudication counts. In RQ1a, 789 games were adjudicated as drawn and 1{,}022 as won, 498 by the hybrid and 524 by AB(8). The protocol's re-run of one RQ1a block without adjudication has not been run for this version. \textbf{Move overhead.} The \MF{} arms run with a 50 ms move overhead and Stockfish 19 with its 10 ms default, so in RQ5c the \MF{} arm has 40 ms less per move; this favours Stockfish 19 slightly and is part of that cell as pre-registered, as are its PGO build against \MF{}'s LTO build. \textbf{Clock division.} Neither the CPU time of each thread nor the effect of the position classifier on the coordinator's budget was measured. Both arms play on the same game clock, which is the resource the comparison holds equal, but they divide it differently: on transformer-enabled moves the hint snapshot and the verification sub-search run before Stockfish's time manager starts timing the move, so such a move can take longer than Stockfish would allot on that clock, and the verification replaces state that the time manager carries from one search to the next. The extra time is drawn from the hybrid's own clock and is part of the system under test; the time of every move is recorded in the released games. \textbf{Power and energy.} CPU and GPU power and energy per game were not measured, because the tools need administrator rights on this host; the fair-resource policy counts threads and wall-clock time, not energy. \textbf{Shared host.} The machine was not idle. The other work was a virtual machine, Python jobs (among them this study's runner and analysis scripts), an editor, an agent gateway and system services such as media analysis and Spotlight indexing: in the 1{,}076 minutes of play above three cores of outside load, the largest outside process was the virtual machine in 251 and a Python process in 397. The gate counted CPU only, and Neural Engine use was not logged. From 27 October the GPU and memory were logged as well: in the cells whose arms do not use the GPU, other processes kept it busy a median of 1 to 2\% of the time (95th percentile up to 50\%, in RQ2a), and memory available never fell below 35\% during a cell's games. Over the \HostLogSamples{} minutes logged from \HostLogFirst{} to \HostLogLast{}, other processes used a median of \HostLogMedianCores{} cores and more than three cores in \HostLogOverPct\% of minutes; over the \HostGameSamples{} minutes that overlapped a game, the median was \HostGameMedianCores{} cores (95th percentile \HostGamePninetyfiveCores{}) and \HostGameOverPct\% of those minutes exceeded three cores (the gate checks the load only before a chunk starts, and until amendment~10 the watchdog stopped a chunk only above 4.5 cores). Because the arms of RQ1a and RQ2 need different numbers of free cores, load can bias as well as blur them: time forfeits came in minutes of high outside load wherever the load was logged, and fell mostly on the alpha-beta arm, which favours the hybrid in RQ1a (amendment~10; Table~\ref{tab:health}); every cell is therefore reported with and without the pairs that overlapped a minute of more than three cores of outside load (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). \textbf{System updates.} The engine binary and its bundled libraries never changed, but the operating system it ran on did: four builds of macOS 27.2 were in use over the measurement period, installed automatically. RQ1a ran on the first three; the last update, which forced a restart, came during RQ2a's last block and caused one time forfeit in it; the rest of that block and the last blocks of RQ2b and RQ5c were played on the final release. The transformer runs on system frameworks (Metal and MPSGraph), so a build change could alter its speed; the cells are reported by build and the microbenchmarks with their builds. \textbf{Interruptions.} RQ1a was restarted once on a rebuilt binary, and the cells were paused and resumed many times by the load gate and by host reboots (Section~\ref{sec:amend}); per-block results are reported so that any effect of the gaps is visible. \textbf{The runners.} The runner cells ran on virtual machines whose neighbours on the same physical host cannot be observed, with a paravirtualised GPU and the virtual machine's timing jitter; fastchess forgave clock overruns of up to one second, and each cell is also reported under the registered rule without that margin. With three cores, the shipped hybrid's MCTS worker competes with alpha-beta for a core, as it did not on the workstation, where four efficiency cores were left beyond the eight threads, so the runner cells measure the hybrid under more contention. The runner images changed during the campaign (two macOS~26 builds), and each cell is reported by image. In RQ5a-R both engines held the network, and the runner swapped in every block; the hash had been halved so that two \MF{} processes would not page, and the other runner cells did not. Runner and workstation cells are not compared with each other. \textbf{Oracle judges.} Stockfish 19 shares the alpha-beta candidate's search and evaluation and Lc0 with BT4 the MCTS candidate's network, so each judge may favour its own lineage; the oracle study reports both judges and their agreement. \textbf{Statistics.} One confirmatory test at fixed $N$; everything else is exploratory and reported in full.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On one M2 Max at 30+0.3, running Lc0's BT4 transformer in an MCTS search on the GPU beside Stockfish~19's
alpha-beta search did not make the engine detectably stronger than the alpha-beta search alone at the
same thread setting: the interval of $[-10.7, +2.0]$~Elo excludes gains above 2~Elo. At 60+0.6, where
the transformer was enabled on 83\% of the moves, the hybrid was weaker than the alpha-beta search alone,
by 40.5~Elo (95\% interval $[-49.6, -31.4]$; exploratory). The arbiter almost
never played the MCTS move, so what the GPU search can change runs through the alpha-beta search it
accompanies: the root hints, the verification sub-search, the agreement rule, and contention for the
shared cores, memory and clock. The split configuration shows that the hybrid as built can cost strength
even with at least as many alpha-beta threads as its opponent on every move: it lost 17~Elo, and its
clock use, the agreement rule's firing rate and its thread-pool rebuilds differed from those of the
shipped hybrid, which kept all eight threads and stayed within the interval above; which of these
differences carries the loss is not separated here.

On three-core virtual M1 machines (the exploratory runner cells), the shipped hybrid lost to alpha-beta at
the same thread setting at both time controls tested: by 35.9~Elo at 30+0.3, and by 16.7~Elo at 10+0.1
while playing alpha-beta's move on every move. The hint and verification channel made no detectable
difference, and the split hybrid's MCTS worker, with a core of its own, added nothing detectable to one
alpha-beta thread. The runners leave no spare core for the MCTS worker, a harder setting than the
workstation's, and their cells are not compared with the workstation's. The hybrid lost even when every
move it played was alpha-beta's, so on such a machine the hybrid around the alpha-beta search costs
strength even when it does not change the move; which of its parts costs it is not separated here.

The GPU search measured here was under-provisioned by design: one worker, batch size~1, a median of 58
evaluations per second inside games at 30+0.3 and 87 at 60+0.6 against a batch-1 ceiling of 139 and a batched throughput near 540,
and an arbiter that defers to alpha-beta unless a predicate fires. A concurrent GPU search is worth
reconsidering when the GPU side is driven harder (batched evaluations, several workers, a search that
does not wait on one evaluation at a time), when it does not take cores the alpha-beta search would
otherwise use, and when an arbiter has evidence that the MCTS move is right often enough to override; the
oracle study, which gives a judge-based bound on what any arbiter between the two root candidates could gain, is the test of
the last condition: \pending{the oracle study's result on the runners (RQ6b-R)}. The 60+0.6 result adds the coordination rules
to these conditions: there the agreement rule ended nearly half of the transformer-enabled moves early,
and no cell of this study switched it off. The engine's CUDA build shares the search and
network contracts, so the same protocol can be run on a discrete GPU, where memory is not shared and the
contention differs; the absolute numbers here do not transfer. If the oracle study finds value in the
disagreements, an arbiter learned from such positions is the natural next step.

\section{Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}
\begin{description}
  \item[Time control 30+0.3.] Thirty seconds per player for the game, plus 0.3~s added after each of that player's moves.
  \item[Nodes, playouts, evaluations.] An alpha-beta node is a position visited by the search; an MCTS playout is one descent from the root to a leaf; an evaluation is one network inference, and a playout can reuse a cached evaluation.
  \item[Fail-high.] A search result at or above the upper bound of its search window; it is unproven until a re-search with a wider window confirms it.
  \item[Bench signature.] The node count of an engine's fixed benchmark search; identical counts mean identical search behaviour on those positions.
  \item[PUCT and first-play urgency.] PUCT selects the child that maximises its value plus an exploration term weighted by the network's prior; first-play urgency is the value assumed for children that have not been visited yet.
  \item[Multi-PV.] A search that reports the best $k$ moves with their scores instead of one.
  \item[Lock convoy.] A collapse of throughput in which threads queue repeatedly on one contended lock.
  \item[HalfKAv2\_hm, FullThreats.] Names of input feature sets of Stockfish's NNUE networks.
  \item[Transformer-enabled fraction.] The share of the hybrid's moves on which the time-safety rule leaves the transformer search on.
  \item[Adjudication, time forfeit.] A game ended by the match manager's draw, resignation or move-cap rule; a game lost by exceeding the clock.
  \item[Syzygy tablebases.] Exact databases of endgame positions with few pieces, used here only by the oracle judges.
  \item[Root hints and verification.] Moves passed from the MCTS root to the alpha-beta root at the start of a move; they set the order of alpha-beta's first iteration, and a short restricted alpha-beta search checks the first five of them.
  \item[Veto.] The arbiter's rule that blocks the MCTS move when alpha-beta, having completed depth 10, ranks it clearly below its own move; most override predicates can lift it (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}).
\end{description}

\section{The arbiter as frozen}
\label{app:ladder}
This appendix lists the decision rules of the frozen engine (source commit \texttt{365f36a}, \texttt{src/hybrid/hybrid\_search.cpp}) under the settings of the hybrid arms. Notation: $R$ is the number of visits the MCTS root received in this move (visits reused from the previous move's tree excluded) and $R_{\mathrm{tot}}$ the number including them; $B$ is the visits of the MCTS move in this move and $S=B/R$; $G$ is the value gap between the MCTS move and the best other move at the root; $c=300\,\mathrm{artanh}\,Q$ is the MCTS move's score in centipawns and $\Delta=c-s_{\mathrm{AB}}$, where $s_{\mathrm{AB}}$ is alpha-beta's score; $Q_g$ is the value gap between the MCTS move and alpha-beta's move in the MCTS statistics. \emph{Sane} means the visit counts are consistent with the playouts and evaluations; \emph{strong} means $R\ge 180$, $B\ge 72$, $S\ge 0.24$, and $B\ge 512$ or $S\ge 0.55$; \emph{clear} means alpha-beta completed depth 10 with $|s_{\mathrm{AB}}|\ge 15$. Every rule also requires a clock-managed search with a positive budget, which holds in all the matches (several names say ``fixed budget'' for historical reasons). Alpha-beta's root scores are in Stockfish's internal units (about three per centipawn) while $s_{\mathrm{AB}}$ and $\Delta$ are in centipawns, so the thresholds mix units. Most rules carry further conditions on the ranks of the two moves in each other's lists and on alpha-beta's effort; the table gives the main ones, and the arbiter reference gives all of them.

The veto holds when alpha-beta has completed depth 10, ranks its own move first and lists the MCTS move at rank 2 or lower among its first 16 root moves (an MCTS move outside that list is never vetoed), and either its average score for the MCTS move is at least 30 internal units below its own move's after at least 10{,}000 nodes on its own move, or it left the MCTS move without an exact score while spending at least ten times as many nodes on its own. The last column gives each rule's relation to the veto: \emph{lifts} (the rule overrides it), \emph{blocked} (the rule cannot fire while it holds), or the stricter condition under which the rule lifts it.
